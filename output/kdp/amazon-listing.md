## Description — ready to paste

**What if the economy's biggest winners make it harder to discover what everyone else could have achieved?**

In an online music experiment, a song called Lockdown finished first in one parallel market and fortieth in another. The recording was the same. So were the competing songs. What changed was the history of who listened and what they saw other people choosing.

That result opens Martin Erlic's investigation into how markets reward success—and how success changes the evidence we use to explain it. An early contract brings references. A popular platform attracts more users. A leading AI company gains customers and resources for its next investment. Each victory can change who gets a meaningful chance next.

At the center of Shadow Futures is the comparison budget: the accumulated chance that opportunities go to someone other than the current favorite. Shadow futures are the alternative histories an economy could have produced from the same starting conditions, under the same economic rules. Erlic uses these ideas to investigate when even a growing record of transactions cannot settle how much work, quality, or investment contributed to success.

From Adam Smith's pin factory and Bell's telephone network to Google's search defaults and AI data centers, the book follows a question with growing stakes: what happens when the institutions that select winners also control the opportunities to test alternatives? Its concern is epistemic monopoly: control over the comparisons needed to judge competing possibilities.

The argument reaches into artificial intelligence, automation, and the future of work. Who owns the wealth produced by an AI workforce? What can market rewards tell us about merit and economic inequality? When should antitrust protect opportunities for discovery, even when prices are low? What would justify taxation when rewards alone cannot establish individual contribution?

Inspired by the accessible economics of Bryan Caplan, Matt Ridley, Alex Tabarrok, and Tyler Cowen, and by Michael Lewis's narrative nonfiction, Erlic writes without equations for economists, policymakers, researchers, and curious readers. He offers a new framework for thinking about capitalism, competition, and the evidence behind our judgments of who deserves what.

**An economy's future depends on the alternatives it still gives itself a chance to discover.**
