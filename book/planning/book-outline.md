# Shadow Futures Book Outline

Martin Erlic   16 October 2026

An 80000 word popular economics book about what reinforced markets can reveal about contribution and how institutions can preserve useful comparison. The argument moves from one song's contrasting outcomes to the choices governing AI, work, competition, and taxation. The book uses published research and public records. Its first draft requires no interviews.

## The narrative throughline

The recurring documented object is Lockdown by 52Metro. Its first-place and fortieth-place outcomes in MusicLab frame the prologue and return in Chapters 1, 3, 6, and 12. Salganik's own account supplies the specific rankings. The story follows what the observer can infer in each setting; the book invents no experiences or reactions for the musicians.

Aster and Birch are recurring fictional software suppliers. Their first contract introduces feedback; procurement reveals the comparison budget; AI deployment raises the stakes; the last chapters compare institutional remedies. Every appearance is labeled as a thought experiment. Changing the early awards while holding economic rules fixed is a replay. Changing an input or policy is a separate intervention.

The real-case sequence is fixed: MusicLab, the success-breeds-success field experiments, Bell's 1956 licensing decree, Google search distribution and remedies, AI infrastructure, customer-support deployment, METR's developer studies, and Rothamsted. Each case supplies evidence for a stated mechanism or institutional choice. None is presented as an empirical proof of an infinite-horizon theorem.

The historical throughline asks how far a judgment travels. Smith's pins and water transport lead through steel integration and Bell's telephone network to global platforms, portable ratings, and AI gatekeeping. Use the conditional two-town thought experiment repeatedly: another destination can cease to be another evaluator. The book does not presume old markets were uniformly freer or prove an inevitable decline in comparison. It asks whether synchronized selection changes contestability in kind.

## Opening and word budget

Prologue about 2500 words: Lockdown's two outcomes frame a sequence of documented cases. Kickstarter's randomly assigned first donations introduce early advantage; Bell's 1956 patent opening gives access and unrealized invention a concrete form; customer-support gains and METR's changing developer experiment introduce AI, work, and the difficulty of preserving comparisons. Return to those cases for rewards and taxation, then use Rothamsted's continuing field experiments to introduce the constructive institutional question before ending at Lockdown. Define the comparison budget and shadow futures briefly where the cases make them useful. Keep detailed literature and theorem conditions in notes and later chapters. Introduction 3500 words: the attribution question and the three defined concepts. Chapters 1 to 12 total 72000 words. Epilogue 2000 words: return to the song and the final procurement decision. Notes and references are additional.

The four movements are learning the concepts in Chapters 1 to 3; history, power, and desert in Chapters 4 to 6; AI and work in Chapters 7 to 9; and institutions in Chapters 10 to 12. The author narrates documented choices and explains mechanisms. Sensory scenes, dialogue, motives, and private experiences require an identified record.

## 1 The winner and the explanation

Open with the two rankings of Lockdown, already established in the prologue. Examine the explanation a reader might give after seeing only the winning world. The same song's contrasting outcomes make the difference between appeal and realized success concrete without claiming that quality is irrelevant.

Move to van de Rijt and colleagues' Kickstarter experiment: researchers selected 200 new unfunded projects and donated to 100 chosen at random. Follow its comparison of subsequent support. Retain the paper's finding of diminishing returns to larger initial advantages, which limits a simple runaway-success story. Introduce Merton's Matthew effect and Akerlof's hidden-quality problem as distinct predecessors. Ask what would distinguish productive inputs from the effect of having already succeeded.

Aster and Birch first appear in a short, labeled thought experiment. Both perform real work. One early contract changes references and future access. No invented dialogue or personal history is attached to them. The chapter closes before assigning either supplier an exact share of merit.

V5 citations: Salganik, Dodds, and Watts (2006); van de Rijt et al. (2014); Merton (1968); Akerlof (1970). Added source: Salganik's first-person MusicLab account.

## 2 The comparison budget

Stay with the fictional procurement desk choosing Aster or Birch. Follow a small sequence of awards and state the model-assumed chance of an award outside the current favorite in ordinary words. Add those chances conceptually, with no formula. Distinguish the modeled chances from market shares, realized wins, and a count of independent trials.

Explain why mostly repeating the favored choice may supply little information about input effects, even while the service improves and transactions multiply. Define the particular information question before describing its scarcity. A high comparison budget is an opportunity for information, not a guarantee of identification.

Give two endings. In one, a persistent chance remains and comparison continues accumulating. In the other, chances diminish fast enough for their lifetime total to be finite. Describe strong reinforcement as one sufficient setting, crediting the earlier reinforced-process literature. Close with the cost of allocating some contracts to less certain suppliers.

V5 citations: Bar-Yam (2016); Pemantle (2007); Oliveira (2009); Gottfried and Grosskinsky (2024); Kasy and Sautmann (2021). V5 Sections 2 and 3 and Appendix D.

## 3 Shadow futures

Replay the procurement example with the same underlying parameters, starting state, and response rules, but different early awards. Let later behavior change only as the unchanged rules prescribe. Separate these histories from an intervention that changes an input or a policy. Return briefly to MusicLab as an observed analogue of parallel histories, not a test of the theorem.

Use Le Goff and Soulier's ant-fork experiment as a concrete predecessor: a continued reinforced path differs from repeated finite trials. John Snow's 1854 comparison of intermingled London water-company customers supplies a historical counterweight: useful causal variation can exist within one observed history. Do not identify either case with a measured comparison budget or an empirical proof of the lifetime result.

Explain the finite-budget result in prose and preserve its conditions. Candidate explanations must use the common recorded experiment, retain the same possible next recipients, and differ in a way controlled by remaining comparison. Additional informative outcomes belong in the experiment. The conclusion is a failure of universal consistent recovery, not identical probabilities, total ignorance, or a guarantee that every observed market is permanently unknowable.

Use Blackwell and Dubins to explain how forecasts can converge while attribution remains unresolved in v5's setting. Credit Le Goff and Soulier and Zhang for prior estimation and prediction results. A single grouped technical endnote credits Kakutani, Kabanov and colleagues, Jacod and Shiryaev, Gabriyelyan, and Le Cam and Yang. Close with what an independent, informative replication could add.

V5 citations: Blackwell and Dubins (1962); Le Goff and Soulier (2017); Zhang (2004); Kakutani (1948); Kabanov, Liptser, and Shiryaev (1977); Jacod and Shiryaev (2003); Gabriyelyan (2011); Le Cam and Yang (2000). V5 Appendices A to E.

## 4 The histories that became infrastructure

Open with the two-town carpenter thought experiment, Smith's pins/market extent/water transport, and his account of guild and settlement restrictions. The 1920 United States Steel opinion prevents a false story in which physical industry never reached enormous scale. Credit Anderson's 1972 More Is Different as an analogy and note his Bell affiliation. Then make Bell's 1956 patent licensing decree the sustained historical case and follow the published study of subsequent invention. Explain the study's finding that innovation rose outside telecommunications, together with its different result inside that sector. Do not collapse the 1956 decree into the later Bell breakup.

Follow the route from the 1947 transistor to the 1952 licensing symposium and the 1956 decree, preserving that chronology. Use the 1956 Hush-A-Phone opinion, including years of procedural delay and its permission-asymmetry footnote, and the 1968 Carterfone decision with revisable standards/owner responsibilities. Sony's attributed corporate account of Iwama's reports and the failed TR-52 casing supplies an actual outside-development sequence before 1956. Bardeen's failed field-effect work supplies concrete internal discovery. AT&T's 1955 annual report supplies its own integration defense and an actual transistor-and-solar trial in Americus. Attribute company predictions and distinguish them from independent results.

Connect this documented access change to Arthur's account of increasing returns and historical selection. Put the argument in dialogue with Alchian's 1950 account of profit-based selection under uncertainty: selection and recovery of productive contribution are different questions. Credit David's QWERTY paper in a short note acknowledging that the keyboard's empirical interpretation is disputed. Merton supplies another established account of cumulative advantage.

The interpretive question is whether institutions permit alternatives to develop usable records. Bell's innovation findings do not estimate its comparison budget or establish a finite lifetime budget. Bring back Birch as a supplier gaining a route to demonstrate its capabilities. Close by distinguishing access to existing knowledge from opportunities to produce new comparative evidence.

V5 citations: Arthur (1989); David (1985); Merton (1968); van de Rijt et al. (2014); Hayek (2002). Added sources: Smith (1776), Book I, Chapters I, III and X; United States Steel (1920), pp. 438–439; Anderson (1972); Alchian (1950); Watzinger, Fackler, Nagler, and Schnitzer (2020).

## 5 Who gets to be tested

Open with the publicly documented Google search distribution dispute and the September 2025 remedies involving certain data and syndication access. Separate allegations, findings, remedies, and the book's economic interpretation. Use the dated court record; check subsequent litigation when the chapter is drafted.

Use the actual 2014–2017 Firefox/Yahoo default switch, Google's Bing latency comparison and Project Folly, Mozilla's funding position, and the December 2025 judgment's distinct distribution/data/syndication provisions. The September 2026 implementation report and July 2026 appellate brief are dated party records, not proof of final results. Mozilla's 2023 simulated-device study supplies the same-five-browser timing contrast: rounded preset selection of 11% during setup versus 19% after clicking its icon. Name Samsung Internet/Edge as presets and Chrome as an alternative; timing is randomized, the preceding-click mechanism remains interpretation, and long-run search effects are not measured. The original 2020 dicamba opinion corroborates Meghani's example of unavailable independent testing before the 2016 registration; distinguish that historical detail from the grounds of vacatur and current product status.

Credit Zahra Meghani before introducing the narrower use of epistemic monopoly: control over comparison production relative to a defined contribution question and information set. Her knowledge-governance argument is an antecedent. The book does not claim to have coined the term. Contrast control of relevant trials with possession of a large dataset.

Return to the two towns as a comparison between separate buyers and storefronts sharing one selection system. Use Matthew 2 as a labeled literary analogy for preemption; Cunningham, Ederer, and Ma (2021) supply the actual pharmaceutical acquisition evidence. No historical corroboration or corporate moral equivalence is claimed, and the Google findings remain separate. Then consider a single operator that maintains genuinely informative pilots. These thought experiments explain why organization count and comparison opportunities differ. Close by asking who can check whether an incumbent's private experiments answer the public's consequential questions.

V5 citations: Meghani (2021); Hayek (2002). V5 Sections 4 and 5. Added sources: Google's search case documents and DOJ's September 2, 2025 remedies summary.

## 6 Who deserves the winnings

Return to the composer of Lockdown only through the documented recording and experimental rankings. Do not invent the musician's reaction. Ask what a reward establishes about effort, appeal, luck, and position when we have seen only one resulting market.

Separate causal contribution, contractual entitlement, incentive design, and moral desert. Use entrepreneurship as experimentation to explain why large prospective prizes can be useful. Explain how established tax models address effort, bargaining, and rent-seeking without requiring an exact causal audit of every person's income.

Apply the distinction to Aster's fictional owners and Birch's workers. Both can have acted productively even when their rewards diverge. The book argues for care in retrospective attribution and then states its normative choices openly. It does not infer an optimal tax rate, equal merit, or an exact luck percentage. Close by moving from the moral defense of winners to the physical investment needed for AI.

V5 citations: Kerr, Nanda, and Rhodes-Kropf (2014); Piketty, Saez, and Stantcheva (2014); Rothschild and Scheuer (2016); Merton (1968); Salganik, Dodds, and Watts (2006).

## 7 The intelligence factory

Build the opening from the IEA's documented data-center infrastructure account, without claiming to have visited a facility. Follow the physical requirements of a computing cluster: accelerators, network capacity, power, cooling, and capital. Use Stanford's 2026 AI Index for the industry context. Date all investment and model-capability claims.

Explain frontier training, post-training, and inference as different economic activities. Frontier describes the leading capability at a given date, not an enduring group of firms. Scale can generate useful capability and lower costs while raising the expense of independent entry. Keep that tension rather than asserting that a large training run proves monopoly or information destruction.

Aster and Birch now purchase AI capacity under explicitly specified hypothetical arrangements. Compare access to a common infrastructure service with an exclusive route to users. Arrow supplies the established appropriation problem, and entrepreneurship research supplies the incentive argument. Close by following a trained system from the factory into repeated use.

V5 citation: Kerr, Nanda, and Rhodes-Kropf (2014). Added sources: Arrow (1962); Stanford AI Index (2026); IEA Key Questions on Energy and AI (2026).

## 8 A billion answers

Follow an explicitly hypothetical inference request through the supplier choice, generated answer, user response, and next routing decision. Repetition can reveal useful performance on encountered tasks. It need not identify the contribution of inputs or the outcome of an alternative training and distribution history.

Distinguish evaluating fixed models on comparable tasks from learning the returns to different economic institutions. Shared model ancestry and a shared ranking can make seemingly separate services dependent. Conversely, routing experiments and independent deployment can add informative evidence. Logged routing probabilities and informative reward labels must be modeled; the allocation-only theorem cannot simply be applied after those observations are ignored.

Return to the prediction and explanation distinction, crediting Blackwell and Dubins and Zhang. Give AI's positive case equal analytical weight: it may make independent tests cheaper. Simulated worlds can test implications of assumptions; their realism requires external validation. Close with the people whose work supplies training examples and whose jobs change when deployment expands.

V5 citations: Bar-Yam (2016); Blackwell and Dubins (1962); Zhang (2004). V5 Section 3. Added context: Stanford AI Index (2026).

## 9 The first rung

Commit to the published customer-support deployment studied by Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond. Explain its average productivity gain and the different effects across workers, including evidence of learning. Follow the observed workplace design; invent no worker dialogue. Distinguish task output from wages, headcount, and an economy-wide employment effect.

Then turn to METR's early-2025 developer experiment and its February 2026 account of changing selection into the follow-up study. Developers sometimes avoided participation or tasks that might require working without AI; lower pay and time measurement also affected interpretation. The lesson is that adoption can change which comparisons are available. It is not a demonstration that v5's finite-budget conditions hold.

Use ILO (2021) platform-work evidence, including observed alternative-platform use after deactivation, and the hypothetical moving driver to ask whether another town still offers another judge. Add the 2024 EU directive's ratings portability and December 2026 transposition deadline; distinguish enacted provisions from implementation. Pair proposed portability with correction, contextual interpretation, appeal, and independent assessment. Then use Birch's hypothetical junior staff to investigate a future question: can automating initial tasks remove routes for developing and demonstrating expertise? The customer-support evidence also supports the possibility of faster learning. Close with a concrete institutional choice between monitored augmentation, immediate replacement, and preserving a limited comparison group, with their costs stated.

Added sources: Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond (2025, published version); METR early-2025 trial; METR update of February 24, 2026. V5 supplies the question, not an empirical finding about labor markets.

## 10 Competition for future evidence

Revisit the Google search remedies and Bell's licensing experience. State that competition policy already considers quality, innovation, entry, and labor conditions. The additional proposed rationale concerns the production of evidence useful for a specified decision. Economic desirability and legal authority remain separate questions.

Commit to a fictional public buyer choosing between Aster and Birch for an AI-assisted service. Specify eligible suppliers, comparable tasks, outcome definitions, retention of trial records, and a purchasing decision. Compare an audit, portability, a limited pilot, and independent procurement routes. Blackwell helps explain decision value; Kasy and Sautmann explain why adaptive allocation involves a learning choice.

Judge remedies by attainable decision improvement, implementation costs, scale benefits, and uncertainty about how to interpret the evidence. Several trials sharing one uncalibrated measure can fail together. Credit Meghani and Hayek for antecedents. Use the explicit hypothetical of OpenAI as sole foundational supplier plus gatekeeper of essential development, distribution, and evaluation. State why sole supply alone is insufficient for total epistemic monopoly, and why market clearing and many agents do not guarantee an independently developed rival. Commit to a rebuttable access presumption where credible irreversible foreclosure threatens useful testing, with independent review, proportionate costs, privacy/security safeguards, and research incentives. Arrow and Fisher (1974) supply the option-preservation antecedent; DMA Articles 7 and 6(11) provide scoped legal precedents. This is a normative proposal requiring empirical support and lawful authority.

V5 citations: Meghani (2021); Hayek (2002); Kasy and Sautmann (2021). Added foundation: Blackwell (1953). Policy extension: Erlic's unpublished Competition Policy for Informative Market Access draft. Added legal source: DOJ 2023 Merger Guidelines, Guideline 2.

## 11 Taxing lost comparison

Begin with the fictional buyer's expense of replacing a lost independent supplier trial. Connect local versus network-wide foreclosure to feasible replacement: another town may supply a substitute, while a shared gate may require rebuilding an institution. Treat the contrast conditionally, without assigning a real tax or measured harm. Introduce no real-company tax estimate. State the separate v5 argument: when hidden position can offset a changed input effect, identical observable laws can assign different contributions to a reward. A rule using that record cannot implement an exact earned-versus-positional decomposition in every such economy.

Connect this limit to established public-finance work without claiming it refutes optimal taxation. State the chosen distributional principles separately. Then develop the additional corrective-tax argument from a defined action to lost evidence, a worse consequential decision, and an external cost that private incentives fail to internalize.

Compare an access remedy, a testing payment, and funded evaluation. Retain the companion draft's insights that public trials can crowd out private testing and that feasible replacement can limit the cost of lost comparison. Compute or profit alone is an unreliable proxy for this harm. Close by explaining which measurements would be necessary before choosing a real instrument or rate.

V5 citations: Piketty, Saez, and Stantcheva (2014); Rothschild and Scheuer (2016); Kerr, Nanda, and Rhodes-Kropf (2014). Added foundation: Arrow (1962). Extension: Erlic's unpublished Taxing Lost Comparison draft, whose additional model assumptions must be stated.

## 12 An economics of possible worlds

Open at Rothamsted's documented long-term agricultural experiments. Explain the institutional commitment needed to preserve useful comparisons while adapting experiments to changing farming conditions. This is a constructive historical example of producing evidence, not an application of v5's theorem to wheat.

Return to Aster and Birch and choose a costed, time-limited evaluation design rather than indefinite exploration. Explain how experimental access, outcome definitions, compatible records, and independent evaluation can work together. Recognize the established economics of learning, counterfactual analysis, discovery, and experimental design.

End with Lockdown. In the experimental setting its alternative outcomes were observed; in ordinary markets many alternatives remain unrealized. The proposal is to measure and preserve valuable comparison opportunities where feasible. The reader should leave able to distinguish an observed winner, an explanation of contribution, and a justified institutional choice. The epilogue returns to that distinction in one final procurement decision.

V5 citations: Hayek (2002); Kasy and Sautmann (2021); Blackwell and Dubins (1962); Salganik, Dodds, and Watts (2006). Added sources: Rothamsted's history and account of adapting its experiments; Blackwell (1953).

## The earlier work on information and uncertainty

### Alchian in 1950

Uncertainty, Evolution, and Economic Theory is the closest title match to the remembered 1950s paper. Alchian explains how realized profits can select behavior even without individually optimal foresight. Shadow Futures asks what a resulting history can reveal about productive contributions when past rewards alter later opportunities. This is a further attribution question, not a general refutation of market selection. Alchian is an added reference, absent from v5's 24-item bibliography.

### Blackwell in 1953

Equivalent Comparisons of Experiments gives a decision-based way to compare experiments. Roughly, one experiment is at least as informative as another when it permits at least as good a decision across the theorem's decision problems, with a corresponding statistical transformation characterization. This is a foundation for valuing preserved comparison, not a theorem that more observations always justify their cost. It is an addition to the book's bibliography, not one of v5's 24 references.

### Blackwell and Dubins in 1962

Merging of Opinions with Increasing Information is the direct classical source cited in v5. Under its absolute-continuity condition, conditional predictions merge as information accumulates. It does not say everyone converges to truth or that causal effects become identifiable. V5 combines this result with its particular single-history allocation limit.

### Arrow in 1962

Economic Welfare and the Allocation of Resources for Invention studies knowledge production under uncertainty and problems of appropriating its benefits. It supports the welfare discussion about private incentives and useful information. It does not establish the comparison budget or price its depletion. This is an added book reference.

### Arrow in 1963

Uncertainty and the Welfare Economics of Medical Care examines how uncertainty about illness and treatment, information differences, and risk-bearing shape medical institutions. This is another uncertainty classic from the period. It is a comparison for the institutional argument, not evidence that healthcare satisfies v5's theorem. It is an added book reference.

### The wider lineage

Knight's Risk, Uncertainty and Profit is from 1921, outside the 1950s and 1960s. Hayek's competition-as-discovery lecture dates to 1968; v5 cites the 2002 translation. Merton's Matthew effect is from 1968. Kakutani's mathematical predecessor is from 1948. Akerlof's lemons paper is from 1970. These are different contributions and should not be compressed into a claim that uncertainty or market learning was previously overlooked.

## The citation standard

All 24 references in the canonical October 2026 v5 are retained below and assigned to chapters in research/v5_reference_register.json. Relevant antecedents receive author-date attribution in the explanatory text or a specific endnote. Grouped notes credit mathematical sources without introducing equations. A planned location is not a completed verification of a source or a drafted chapter.

Before a chapter's factual or novelty claim is finalized, check the relevant full source and add its page or section to the working citation record. Identify unpublished Erlic extensions as unpublished drafts and state their additional assumptions. Preserve the distinction between a theoretical result, an empirical finding, an illustrative analogy, and a policy proposal. Every v5 source has a substantive role or technical-lineage note; none is silently omitted.
