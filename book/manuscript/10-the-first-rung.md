# Chapter 9

## The first rung

On September 10, 2020, an engineer ran a load test and an AI assistant stopped making suggestions. The interruption appears in an unusual place: an economics paper about customer support. Workers continued answering customers while the software that had been helping them was unavailable. A breakdown had created an opportunity to ask what remained after assistance disappeared.[^c9-1]

Usually, a company wants to know whether its new tool works. Does it resolve more complaints? Does it shorten the queue? Does the customer leave satisfied? An outage raises another question. Has the worker acquired something that can survive the disappearance of the machine?

That question belongs near the center of the argument about AI and employment. A worker and a computer can become a more productive team without the worker becoming more capable alone. They can also become a more productive team because the worker learns from the computer. The difference is easy to miss while both are working.

The customer-support study supplies a hopeful case. Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond examined the introduction of a generative assistant among 5,172 agents. In the published study, access raised issues resolved per hour by an average of 15 percent. The gains were concentrated among workers with less experience and lower initial performance. Experienced, highly skilled workers gained much less; some quality measures deteriorated slightly for the strongest workers.[^c9-2]

These were live text conversations about business software. An agent had to understand the customer's problem, find an appropriate response, and communicate it. The assistant suggested language and information. The human could use, change, or ignore its suggestions. The measured improvement belonged to that arrangement: a person handling customers with help available.

But the outage evidence adds something. Previously assisted workers retained improvements in handling speed when suggestions became unavailable. The pattern was consistent with learning, and it became stronger with more prior exposure. It was imperfect evidence: outages were not a clean randomized training experiment, and the conversations handled during them could differ. Still, the machine's temporary absence helped the researchers investigate a benefit that its continuous presence might conceal.[^c9-3]

The first rung of a career need not be protected from AI. In this workplace, AI appeared capable of helping people climb it.

That possibility should make us more demanding about what we measure. If the company had recorded only assisted output, it would have missed part of the question. If an observer had looked only at the reduced gap between beginners and experts, the observer might have concluded that expertise was becoming unimportant. Yet an assistant can transmit useful practices precisely because someone developed those practices in the first place.

Consider the customer's next unusual problem. A quick answer is valuable if it is right. Someone must recognize when a familiar response does not fit, when the customer has supplied a misleading clue, or when an apparent solution creates another problem. A system that helps more people develop that judgment has a different economic future from one that merely makes their ordinary replies sound equally accomplished.

The study cannot settle that future. It did not observe compensation, and it did not establish the long-run response of total employment. Better service might reduce the labor needed for a fixed volume of contacts. It might also make support useful enough that customers seek more of it. Employers could change targets, hours, staffing, or pay. A productivity estimate does not select one of these responses for us.[^c9-4]

The missing wage record matters for a second reason. Suppose a beginner's improved reply draws on patterns learned from excellent workers. The resulting output does not arrive with a receipt dividing its value among the beginner, those earlier workers, the model developer, and the employer that organized the service. The customer has received help. The division of credit remains a further question.

A narrowing performance gap can mean useful knowledge travels farther. Experts might still be crucial for diagnosing unusual failures, developing practices, or improving the system that distributes the old ones. The strongest support workers' smaller gains concern that deployment. They do not establish that a business can dispense with the expertise on which future assistance may depend. A firm needs to examine that continuing work before turning the average gain into a staffing plan.

We should carry both observations forward. A machine can create real value and improve human learning. The reward history of the resulting workplace can still leave important contributions unresolved. Neither observation cancels the other.

## The answer and the ability

A mathematics classroom offers a sharper separation between successful assistance and acquired skill. Hamsa Bastani and her collaborators studied nearly a thousand high-school students in Turkey using two versions of a GPT-based tutor. One resembled a standard chat assistant. The other had instructions designed to support learning, including guidance to offer hints instead of supplying the complete solution.[^c9-5]

The students practiced, then took an assessment without the tool. During practice, both assisted groups performed better than the group using ordinary resources. Once assistance was removed, the students assigned the standard assistant performed worse than the control group. The tutor designed around learning largely avoided that harm. Its striking practice gains did not translate into a clearly established advantage on the subsequent unassisted assessment.

The researchers had measured two different achievements. One was getting through the problem with help. The other was being able to solve a related problem afterward. A completed worksheet could look like evidence of either. The assessment made the distinction visible.

The experiment measured short-run learning with particular tools and tasks. How assistance was organized changed what students could do when it was withdrawn. Following their later working lives would require a different study.

The tutor's design required human work. Teachers supplied problem-specific information, correct solutions, and guidance about common mistakes. The assistant was instructed to ask for the student's attempt and respond to the difficulty. Behind the apparently effortless exchange stood a choice about what the student should still have to do.

The experiment preserved an occasion to find out. Students encountered a teacher's review, practiced under assigned arrangements, then completed a closed-book, closed-laptop assessment graded with a common rubric. Observing later performance prevented the study from ending when an interface first made the task easier. Workplace assessments face the same question: when should the next observation occur?

Buying an answer and supporting learning are different uses of assistance. A tool can complete a task or help a user develop the capacity to handle later tasks. Immediate deliverables can look similar while the consequences for later ability differ.

Neither approach is always preferable. A customer facing an urgent deadline may reasonably want the repair. An employee already familiar with the problem may gain nothing from another lesson. Instruction takes time, and a tool that insists on teaching every experienced user could become a nuisance. The relevant choice concerns the purpose of this task for this person at this stage of development.

The classroom researchers also examined students' messages. With the standard assistant, students often simply asked for the answer. With the learning-oriented tutor, they exchanged more messages and increasingly asked for help or attempted solutions. The authors saw this contrast as consistent with students using the standard assistant to obtain solutions instead of working through problems. It helps us see a possible mechanism. The message patterns do not separately identify the contribution of each safeguard, since the tutor changed several things together.[^c9-5]

Deliverable quality, transfer to an unfamiliar task, and the ability to detect an incorrect recommendation require different assessments. Passing one does not establish the others.

There is no reason to make every assessment an exercise in working without computers. The relevant future skill may be choosing, checking, and combining powerful tools. An accountant need not abandon a spreadsheet to demonstrate professional judgment. A programmer may reasonably use an assistant in the evaluation of programming skill. The test should preserve the ability the employer actually needs to know about.

Assessment should follow the future ability that matters, including recovery when a familiar assistant fails, checking sources, and reviewing output from another system. Preserving a test that answers no useful question can become an expensive ritual.

The first rung is therefore more than a collection of simple tasks. It is an arrangement that lets a beginner attempt something, receive feedback, improve, and demonstrate the improvement. AI can change every part of that arrangement. It can make practice cheaper, feedback quicker, and difficult work accessible earlier. It can also complete the activity from which the beginner was supposed to learn.

## The disappearing control group

The experienced programmers in METR's early-2025 experiment present another surprise. Sixteen developers worked on 246 tasks in open-source projects they knew well. Tasks were specified before assignment to conditions allowing or disallowing AI. With the tools available in that study, allowing AI made completion take 19 percent longer.[^c9-6]

That result had a precise setting. These were experienced contributors in mature projects, facing real tasks and established standards. The tools were those used during the early-2025 study, primarily a coding environment with then-current models. Nineteen percent longer describes task completion time under the experiment's measurement. It does not mean that every programmer became 19 percent less productive, or that later tools must have the same effect.

The developers' expectations pointed in the other direction. They had expected assistance to save time and afterward still believed it had done so. The discrepancy makes a practical point. A tool can feel helpful during individual moments while adding time across the complete job. Waiting, supplying context, examining suggestions, and correcting mistakes all belong in the cost of producing an acceptable result.

METR collected screen recordings and examined a subset in detail. The records made activities such as prompting, waiting for generation, reviewing output, and writing code separately visible. That is a useful antidote to judging a coding assistant by how rapidly text appears. The desired product is an acceptable change in a working project. A suggestion that arrives quickly can still create work before it belongs there. The recordings do not establish a single cause of every slowdown, and the researchers considered multiple possible explanations. They do show how a better measurement can move the discussion from an impression about assistance to the actual sequence of actions required to finish a task.

Set this study beside the customer-support deployment. One finds substantial average gains, especially for novices. The other finds a slowdown among experienced developers in familiar repositories. The interesting question becomes which workers, tasks, tools, and working arrangements produce which result. A single number for the effect of AI would conceal the decisions an employer actually has to make.

In its February 24, 2026 account of a follow-up effort, METR reported difficulty obtaining a trustworthy comparison. Some developers disliked the prospect of being assigned to work without AI. Some tasks were kept out of the experiment. The available observations were changing along with adoption.[^c9-7]

One participant's published comment included the simple explanation, “I really like using AI!” That is an ordinary preference. It is also a research problem. If a developer avoids entering the experiment because a particularly suitable task might land in the no-AI condition, the measured comparison no longer represents the full set of tasks the researcher hoped to study.

Random assignment cannot recover a task that never enters the pool. It can make the allowed and disallowed conditions comparable among eligible, submitted tasks. The researcher must still ask why these tasks were submitted and what happened to the rest. The boundary around the experiment is part of the evidence.

Study pay also fell from $150 to $50 an hour, potentially altering participation. Developers could run agents concurrently, complicating the meaning of time spent on a task. Unfinished work and differences in task selection created further difficulties. These were reasons to reconsider the design, rather than declare that the original slowdown had cleanly reversed into a precisely measured speedup.

The attraction of the tool could be evidence that it had become more useful. It could also reflect convenience, enjoyment, expectations, or the kinds of work people now chose to attempt. METR believed the newer tools likely helped more, while emphasizing how weakly the follow-up identified the magnitude. The preference to use a product and a credible estimate of its productivity effect answer different questions.

Quality, completion time, and workers' experience should be measured separately. A slower method can be preferred for other reasons. Calling every benefit productivity obscures the choice, while disregarding preference discards something workers value.

This problem is familiar outside software. A worker who has reorganized a day around a tool may reasonably resist surrendering it for research. An employer may dislike paying for deliberately slower work. A researcher cannot assume that preserving a clean comparison costs nothing merely because the comparison would be informative.

Adoption can change what a later comparison means. Users develop habits, forget old procedures, and change which tasks they attempt. Returning to an earlier tool then changes more than a suggestion's availability. An evaluation must specify which stage of that adjustment it measures.

Staged introduction, comparison of assisted training methods, and assessment on new tasks can still supply evidence. Each answers a different question. More observations of the adopted workflow cannot automatically recover every earlier comparison.

Here the distinction between activity and informative opportunity becomes tangible. A company might accumulate millions of AI-assisted edits while losing the practical ability to recruit a comparable group for a particular no-AI trial. The edits could be useful evidence about errors, quality, and day-to-day performance. Their number would not, by itself, answer the missing question about the alternative arrangement.

Adoption can make a tool more useful while making a particular experiment harder to run. METR had to redesign its study because the way developers worked had changed.

## From tasks to jobs

Bank tellers offer a historical warning against equating an automated task with an eliminated job. James Bessen reported that the number needed in an average urban branch fell from twenty to thirteen between 1988 and 2004, while urban branch numbers grew by 43 percent. David Autor drew on this account to explain how lower operating costs and expanding demand can change employment. The remaining teller work also shifted toward customer relationships and other services.[^c9-9]

Autor also identified deregulation as part of the branch expansion. The figures do not isolate the cash machine's causal effect. They show why counting automated withdrawals cannot by itself tell us how many people a bank will employ. The business can change the number of outlets, the services it offers, and the work it assigns to people.

Autor's argument concerns relationships among tasks. Automation can increase the value of complementary work, but the employment response depends on demand for that work. Saving time can expand production, improve quality, or reduce labor hours. Identifying an automated step is the start of an employment analysis.

There is harder evidence on the other side. Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo studied exposure to industrial robots across American labor markets over 1990–2007. They found adverse effects on employment and wages in more exposed places. A machine's productivity benefits did not ensure that the affected local workers would gain.[^c9-10]

Their setting involved industrial robots performing work such as assembly, welding, and painting. Their analysis used differences in local exposure based on industry composition and robot adoption, rather than randomly distributing robots among towns. The causal interpretation requires assumptions and checks about other forces affecting those places. It is serious evidence about a historical adjustment, with a defined design and a defined scope.

Notice what this research can accomplish without rerunning the whole American economy. Places entered the period with different industrial structures, making their exposure to robot adoption different. The researchers used that variation and investigated competing explanations. Their estimates remain conditional on the design, but the absence of a literal parallel America does not make historical evidence useless. Shadow futures sharpen the demand to specify which alternative is being studied and where the information comes from. They should not become an excuse to dismiss a carefully constructed comparison merely because it occurs within one history. Here the relevant contrast is supplied by differences across labor markets, with the design's assumptions carrying part of the inferential burden.

The teller account and the robot study should make us impatient with easy reassurance as well as easy catastrophe. Automation can reduce the labor needed for particular work. Lower costs and new activities can create demand elsewhere. The balance, location, timing, and distribution of those responses matter. An aggregate gain can coexist with a damaging local loss.

The first rung adds a further adjustment. Firms need workers today, but they may also need people capable of doing more demanding work later. If the tasks that once introduced beginners to a profession become cheaper to automate, the employer can redesign training, recruit experienced staff, hire fewer entrants, or develop another route into expertise. Those choices affect the supply of skilled labor that future firms will encounter.

Preserving obsolete manual work solely because it once trained beginners can be costly. The constructive question is whether an alternative route develops the needed expertise, and who has an incentive to finance it.

A simulation might provide repeated practice without risking a customer's records. A supervised assignment could expose a beginner to a more demanding diagnosis sooner. The classroom tutor offers a real example of deliberately changing assistance around learning. The customer-support deployment offers evidence consistent with skills spreading through assistance. These possibilities deserve trials alongside the fear that the entry route will disappear.

It is possible, however, for an employer to discover that it can buy experienced judgment elsewhere more cheaply than it can cultivate it. If many employers make that choice, the next generation's opportunities may change. That is a hypothesis about incentives and collective adjustment. It requires evidence about hiring, training, mobility, and demand. It cannot be established by pointing to a machine that writes a good report.

## Who gets the difficult assignment?

Responsibility itself can reinforce opportunity. A successful assignment produces a record that supports the next selection. Employers can rationally rely on that record while earlier allocation decisions partly determine whose ability becomes observable.

Absence of a record differs from a record of failure. Neither guarantees future success, but the evidence available in the two cases is different. Evaluating skill requires distinguishing a poor result from a missing occasion to demonstrate it.

AI could widen access to supervised practice by reducing the cost of checking routine steps. It could also concentrate scarce responsibility among fewer already-tested workers. Which effect occurs depends on hiring, training, assignment, and demand.

Platform work provides documented evidence of selection through a record. The International Labour Organization's 2021 report describes ratings affecting task allocation and access to work, with negative ratings contributing to deactivation.[^c9-platform]

Geographic mobility offers less independence when the same ranking system governs work across locations. If practical alternatives are also unavailable or reuse the same record, moving does not create a new evaluator. This is a hypothesis about dependence, rather than a description of every platform worker.

That last condition matters. The ILO report also records surveyed workers using another platform after permanent deactivation from one. Platforms can open distant demand, and workers can have alternatives. The concern is the situation in which those alternatives become impractical or rely on the same record. We should investigate that dependence rather than assume every worker already faces it.[^c9-platform]

Europe's 2024 Platform Work Directive addresses one part of the problem. It provides for portability of personal data generated through platform work in automated monitoring and decision systems, including ratings and reviews. Its national transposition deadline is December 2, 2026. At the time of writing, the deadline remains ahead; the existence of the directive is not evidence of uniform implementation or successful remedies.[^c9-portability]

A portable reputation can help a good worker leave without starting from nothing. It can also make an unfair rating harder to escape. Carry the same flawed score everywhere and portability spreads the original judgment. My proposed safeguard therefore pairs voluntary export with correction, an explanation of context, appeal, and opportunities for independent assessment. A fresh evaluation is different from a right to conceal fraud.

Independent assessment, correction of disputed ratings, and context-sensitive records can make reputation contestable. Each costs something, and an existing score may often be useful. The relevant freedom is a workable way to challenge the evidence and obtain another meaningful observation.

A supervisor's assessment, a practice task, or a second employer's trial can supply evidence missing from the original rating. A reputation system should leave room for those observations to change its verdict.

The employer still needs deadlines met and people paid. It need not solve perfect merit before organizing work. The first rung can disappear because a task was automated. It can also disappear because one institution's early verdict travels farther than the worker's practical ability to find another judge.

## Buying a future workforce

Employers can automate routine work, redesign entry routes around assistance, or investigate the consequences before changing staffing widely. These choices trade immediate output against the cost and future value of developing judgment.

Automation may be economical where routine work is reliable, demand for human review is limited, and experienced judgment remains available. A thinner entry route does not make the production saving imaginary.

Assisted training may instead support growing demand and develop future reviewers. The customer-support findings make that possibility worth investigating; they do not establish a universal response across occupations.

Evaluation itself consumes supervision, time, and operational resources. Protecting customers and giving participants a fair basis for taking part belong in its cost. A relevant comparison must justify that expense.

The horizon must match the staffing question. A week of faster output cannot establish whether reducing supervised entry positions creates a shortage of reviewers two years later. Skill development and progression require longer observation.

The measurements should fit that decision. Immediate output and error rates matter. So do the ability to handle unfamiliar cases, the supervisor time required, the quality of later reviews, and whether participants progress into roles the business needs. Retention and the movement of trained workers to other employers may affect the private return to training. Measuring everything indiscriminately is less useful than observing what distinguishes the feasible choices.

Intermediate measures can help explain that development: unfamiliar problems handled, errors caught before delivery, and repeated supervisor corrections. They can reveal an impressive early performance that fails to transfer, or a costly beginning that develops valuable judgment.

A useful study defines what evidence would support expansion, revision, or stopping. The classroom experiment demonstrates why polished output and subsequent capability should remain separate outcomes.

METR's experience also warns that selection can change once users develop strong preferences about tools. A credible comparison may require compensation for extra time, different randomization, or a narrower question. A control-group label alone establishes no comparability.

Employees should understand what the test is meant to establish. A trial intended to compare training methods can become distorted if participants reasonably believe that one condition marks them as candidates for dismissal. A firm has to account for the behavior its evaluation creates. That is part of making the evidence useful, as well as part of treating people fairly.

Training can benefit later employers as workers move. That creates a possible gap between private and wider returns, which requires evidence about mobility, costs, and skill formation before it can justify a subsidy.

The same reasoning cuts against preserving a program simply because it trains people. Training can teach obsolete practices, select poorly, or consume supervision needed for more valuable work. An alternative employer, professional course, or well-designed AI tutor might supply a better route. The comparison has to include plausible replacements, rather than treat the existing arrangement as the only imaginable source of skill.

There are limits to what one firm can settle. It cannot observe the entire labor market's response to simultaneous adoption. It may not know how another employer's staffing choices affect the future pool of reviewers. This is a reason for research across firms, careful labor-market evidence, and institutions that can evaluate shared problems. It is not a reason to substitute a confident story for missing observations.

An employer choosing the fastest tool today may be entirely sensible. A workforce, an industry, or a government may have a longer horizon and different interests. Making those interests explicit is more productive than asking whether AI is simply good or bad for labor. The important choices concern who gains, who bears the cost, what can still be learned, and which arrangements remain feasible.

## Who owns the workforce?

The rise of agent work also changes which assets are accumulated. Model calls and checking are operating expenses. Equipment, retained code, reliable tests, and durable productive capabilities may support future production. A large stream of generated work need not leave its purchaser with independently usable assets.

Workers can benefit through wages, ownership claims, and cheaper goods or services. Those are different channels, and their distribution cannot be inferred from a count of agent sessions or completed tasks.

Investment can expand the capacity to attempt new projects or strengthen exclusive control of routes through which others must be tested. Both may involve productive expenditure. Their consequences for future comparison depend on what the resulting assets permit.

A feedback between automated production and reinvestment could help entrants develop or help well-financed incumbents advance faster. Demand, financing, competition, resource constraints, and checking costs determine its strength. A successful agent experiment does not demonstrate an autonomously expanding firm.

Accumulation also faces depreciation, imitation, failures, and rivals. Productive integration can improve services while making independence harder. The relevant assessment includes both those benefits and the feasibility of another development route.

Anton Korinek and Donghyun Suh examine automation and capital accumulation together in their March 2024 scenario model. Investment can complement work that remains with people. Its pace relative to automation and the assumed range of human tasks can produce very different wage paths. Their model also separates reproducible capital, including compute, from fixed scarce factors. It gives no unconditional forecast that wages or monopoly profits must move in one direction.[^c9-capital]

Software cannot instantly create an electricity connection or manufacture servers. Scarcity can constrain expansion, but temporary shortage alone does not establish permanent gatekeeping. The stronger claim needs an identifiable obstruction and evidence about feasible alternatives.

Employee ownership, jointly financed equipment, and public computing access distribute claims and authority differently. Their ability to support independent development depends on financing, operation, and evaluation as well as legal ownership.

Broader ownership does not automatically create independent discovery. A cooperative can depend on one provider's records, while a private owner can finance useful tests. Claims on income and control over learning belong in the same analysis without becoming the same question.

Similarly, income distribution and entrepreneurial opportunity can diverge. Holding a stake in an existing firm does not ensure the ability to start another business, obtain inputs, reach customers, or challenge an inherited evaluation.

The future of work includes the future of assets built through work: who can retain them, redirect them, and use them to attempt something new. Affordable tools and usable routes to demonstrate judgment can change together or move in opposite directions.

These choices depend on institutions as well as contracts. Investment can coexist with closed access, while publicly financed access can become another gate. The next chapter examines the rules governing the practical ability to compete.
