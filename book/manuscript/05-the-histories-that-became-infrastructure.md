# Chapter 4

## The histories that became infrastructure

### How far can a judgment travel?

A market can grow while the number of independent places to try again shrinks.

Imagine a carpenter who disappoints a buyer in one town. Another town might offer a different buyer, unfamiliar with the dispute and willing to commission a small job. Now imagine that both towns purchase through one platform, which carries the carpenter's ranking from one to the other. The second town is still there. The second judgment may have disappeared. The carpenter's ability to start again depends on whether another buyer can make a fresh assessment.

Adam Smith began his account of the division of labor with a pin workshop. Specialization raised what its workers could produce together. Its reach still depended on finding buyers. In his chapter on the extent of the market, Smith contrasted the narrow opportunities of scattered settlements with the commerce made possible by water transport. A larger reachable market could support work that a small village could not.[^c4-smith]

Distance therefore did two things. It limited the customers an artisan could serve, and it could separate the people deciding whether to give that artisan a chance. Removing distance could expand both income and competition. Whether it also joined previously separate judgments is another question. A new road connecting buyers is different from one authority deciding which sellers every connected buyer may see.

The old town was hardly an automatic refuge. Smith also described restrictions imposed by guilds and settlement rules, including obstacles to moving in search of work. A newcomer could meet a closed corporation rather than an open door. History offers no clean passage from free local markets to oppressive global ones. It offers changing combinations of reach, productive scale, and permission.[^c4-smith]

Nor did formidable scale begin with software. By 1920, the Supreme Court's United States Steel opinion was recounting a huge industrial combination and disagreement over whether integration reflected the physical requirements of steel production or suppressed competition. Pig iron and finished steel were physical goods; that had not kept their producers small. Furnaces, transport, capital, and organization could already assemble power over a wide territory.[^c4-steel]

The question is how far a common selection system can reach, and what remains outside it. One intermediary serving ten towns may offer customers more choice than ten isolated villages ever had. It may also carry one exclusion across all ten. Increased scale changes the institutional problem when the route to a fresh evaluation disappears.

In 1972, the physicist Philip Anderson called an essay “More Is Different.” Anderson worked at Bell Laboratories, and his subject was how new levels of physical scale and complexity could require new explanations. His title raises a useful economic question: when does an increase in reach change the kind of independence an economy can sustain?[^c4-anderson]

Consider what is being scaled. A bigger furnace changes the cost of producing steel. A railway changes where steel can be sold. A shared admissions rule changes which producer can reach those buyers at all. These changes can reinforce one another, but they are not interchangeable. The transition to digital markets adds another possibility: an evaluation can travel with the seller and be reproduced across transactions at very little cost. The important change is not that physical limits have vanished. AI's power supplies and computing clusters make those limits conspicuous. It is that physical reach, distribution, and judgment can become organized at different scales, with one ranking governing opportunities across a much wider network.

Bell gives that question a physical object: a telephone attachment that a customer could buy, yet risk losing service by using.

### An invention leaves the laboratory

The transistor existed before the government opened Bell's patents.

In December 1947, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain made a semiconductor device amplify a signal at Bell Laboratories. Their point-contact transistor belonged to a research program drawing on physics, materials, instruments, and the work of several people. In his Nobel lecture, Bardeen emphasized the earlier theoretical work and improvements in materials on which the discovery depended. The invention was an achievement, and it had a history.[^c4-1]

On January 24, 1956, a different kind of event changed what others could do with Bell's inventions. An antitrust settlement required extensive licensing of Bell patents, with thousands of existing patents made available without royalties. The settlement also imposed obligations concerning technical information. It left the Bell System integrated. This was an opening of technology inside a continuing institutional structure, years before the later breakup.[^c4-2]

Between those events lie two questions that are easy to collapse. How did a remarkable invention come into being? What could happen when other people were able to build on it? Bell's accomplishment supplied the starting material for the second question. Recognizing the importance of that accomplishment does not tell us which arrangements would allow the material to produce the most useful subsequent work.

The semiconductor story makes that distinction tangible. A transistor on a laboratory bench is not yet a reliable supply of components, an affordable radio, or a workable design in someone else's product. Moving between those stages requires further decisions and further learning. Whoever gets to undertake that work can discover things the original inventors did not yet know. Whoever cannot undertake it leaves fewer results for later observers to examine.

This is one way a history becomes infrastructure. Earlier work leaves designs, machines, procedures, and skills that make some later projects easier. It can also leave permissions and restrictions that determine who may try them. The resulting advantage can be productive. Engineers using an established body of knowledge may work better because somebody has already paid to develop it. A serious account of opportunity must include that benefit.

Bell's records let us follow both routes. A technology could leave the laboratory through a license and a training program. A finished attachment could reach a shop yet remain barred from a customer's telephone. The invention, the right to build on it, and the right to use the result were separate economic opportunities.

Follow those opportunities rather than asking whether Bell was simply good or bad. One route led from a major laboratory to inventions others could use. Another led from an independent device to a customer whose telephone service could be threatened if the device was attached. The differences between those routes help explain why releasing knowledge and opening a market can have different consequences.

### The price of a license

Bell had shared transistor technology before the 1956 decree. In April 1952, representatives of forty companies attended a nine-day technology symposium after paying a $25,000 patent-licensing fee. The program included a manufacturing visit. The Computer History Museum's account and collection identify this as part of a broader effort to spread semiconductor techniques. Access did not begin on the day of the settlement.[^c4-3]

That chronology prevents a convenient origin story in which the government released a wholly sealed invention and an industry immediately appeared. A licensing fee, a technical gathering, and compulsory licensing represent different arrangements for passing on knowledge. The relevant question is what each arrangement enabled, for whom, at what cost, and with what further restrictions. “Open” is too broad a description to answer all four.

For an engineer, access can require more than permission to practice a patented invention. There may be a difficult production process to understand, a material whose quality matters, or a component that performs differently outside a demonstration. A legal document can remove a restriction without supplying every skill needed to use the resulting freedom. The distinction is familiar to anyone who has followed a recipe and discovered that instructions and competence are different possessions.

The symposium's combination of licensing and instruction is therefore revealing. Making an invention usable can involve transmitting practical knowledge alongside the rights. The economic object reaching another company is a bundle of opportunities and requirements. A cheaper license helps one part of the problem. It need not remove the other costs of turning an idea into something worth buying.

The government's contemporary account of the 1956 judgment recorded a requirement to furnish technical information at a reasonable charge. It also described annual patent lists and a mechanism for settling royalty disagreements. These were ways of making an access obligation operational. A general statement that firms should be allowed to innovate would have left the mechanics unresolved.[^c4-2]

The incumbent has a legitimate interest here too. Producing instructions, answering technical questions, and maintaining a stable interface require work. An access rule can shift those costs onto an organization that does not receive all the resulting benefits. Ignoring that work would make openness look cheaper than it is. Charging for every missing piece without scrutiny could make nominal access unusable. The design has to address both possibilities.

Bell's case thus begins with an economic sequence rather than a single unlocked door: permission, usable knowledge, practical implementation, and a route to a customer. Each stage can fail even when the previous one succeeds. The next case concerns a device that had already reached the stage of manufacture and sale. Its difficulty lay at the telephone itself.

### A cup on a telephone

Hush-A-Phone was a cup-like attachment that fitted onto a telephone. It was intended to provide privacy for the speaker and reduce surrounding noise. The company had been selling it since 1921. By the time its dispute reached the federal appeals court in 1956, more than 125,000 had gone into use, according to the court's account.[^c4-4]

The telephone companies' tariffs prohibited attachments they had not supplied. They claimed the right to suspend or terminate service for violations. Vendors and users were told that Hush-A-Phone could not be used, and some distributors stopped selling it. The independent maker's ability to build a cup had not secured its customers' ability to use one.

The Federal Communications Commission had upheld the restriction. The appeals court examined the distinction between effects on the conversation and harm to the system. Using the cup for maximum privacy could reduce the clarity and volume heard by the other party. But the court saw no findings supporting the claimed injury to the telephone system generally. It set aside the Commission's order and sent the case back, leaving the antitrust questions open.[^c4-4]

This was a dispute about a mundane object, which is precisely why it is useful. We do not need to settle whether Hush-A-Phone was destined to transform communications. Its claim could be considered at the level of what the device did and whom it affected. A privacy aid might involve a trade-off that the two people in a conversation were capable of choosing for themselves.

A subscriber could cup a hand around the mouthpiece to seek privacy. The court asked why an attachment producing a similar effect should be treated differently. The cup also left the hand free to write. A restriction presented as protection of the telephone system reached down to an ordinary choice about how to hold a private conversation.[^c4-4]

A general network cannot function if every user is free to damage everyone else's service. That concern deserves a technical answer. The question is whether a particular restriction follows from a demonstrated harm, a reasonable precaution, or a blanket preference for equipment supplied by the operator. Those possibilities lead to different rules. They should not be made indistinguishable by calling all of them protection of the system.

When a customer cannot use an independently supplied device, the market produces less experience of that use. Fewer people learn whether it is worth the inconvenience, whether a different design works better, or whether another application is possible. A prohibition can affect the record of performance as well as the maker's immediate sales.

The maker had already sold devices; this was no wholly untested invention. Its complaint went to the FCC in December 1948. Hearings followed in 1950, oral argument in 1951, and a final Commission decision only in December 1955. The appeals court noted the four-year delay after argument. Permission was a question about time as well as principle: an independent producer waited while the established system continued to operate. The court's record supports that chronology, not a calculation of the sales or discoveries the delay prevented.[^c4-4]

A footnote exposed the unequal starting positions. Telephone companies could offer equipment unless the Commission stopped them; an independent maker faced authorization before competing. The court called this unfair and said delay aggravated it, while leaving the legal question unresolved. A permission system can allocate time itself: one side continues accumulating customers while the other waits. That interpretation concerns a regulator's procedure as well as private control. It is distinct from the judgment on the cup.[^c4-4]

### What happened after the decree

Decades later, Martin Watzinger, Thomas Fackler, Markus Nagler, and Monika Schnitzer studied invention after Bell's 1956 settlement. They compared follow-on patenting associated with Bell patents with carefully matched non-Bell patents. Their published finding was a lasting increase in innovation outside telecommunications, while the corresponding increase did not appear inside telecommunications, where customer access remained constrained.[^c4-5]

The design matters because a simple before-and-after count would be difficult to interpret. Semiconductor and other technologies were developing for many reasons. A comparison group provides a way to ask whether the patents affected by the licensing change developed differently from otherwise comparable patents. The credibility of that inference depends on the matching, the timing, and the absence of other differences that would account for the result.

The study's measure also has a scope. Follow-on patents and their connections to earlier patents are evidence about a particular dimension of invention. They do not put a monetary value on every resulting product, measure every improvement that went unpatented, or reveal what every unrealized company would have achieved. We can take the finding seriously without turning a patent measure into a complete account of social welfare.

The contrast points to a practical question: can a business actually use the knowledge it is allowed to obtain? A patent license may be worth little if the route to customers remains closed. The Hush-A-Phone dispute makes the telephone network's rules about use tangible.

This does not establish that every obstacle to entry should disappear. A buyer may have sound reasons to require reliability, security, and compatibility. A supplier may fail those requirements even with full access to the underlying knowledge. The valuable distinction is between testing whether an alternative satisfies the requirements and making its failure inevitable through a rule that prevents the test.

Bell's study measures follow-on patenting after a licensing intervention. To investigate the opportunities behind that result, we would also need to know which teams gained access, what work they attempted, and how their chances of reaching users changed.

The decree was an intervention in the economy's rules. It was not a replay of Bell's earlier history with a different lucky first award. That distinction helps explain what its evidence can do. It can inform our understanding of a change in access. It cannot show us every other route the same unchanged economy might have taken, or give each later inventor an exact share of credit for the resulting industry.

A list of patents is a record of inventions already made. A route to a customer can produce evidence about applications that have not yet been tried. Opening the first does not automatically open the second. This is why an institution can distribute a great deal of existing knowledge while leaving important opportunities for future comparison under the control of the same organization.

### An advantage can become useful

One reason these choices matter is that success can improve the thing that succeeds. W. Brian Arthur's 1989 account of competing technologies made this mechanism explicit. Adoption can bring experience and improvement, attracting further adoption. Under the specified increasing-returns conditions, historical events can help select a durable technological outcome that need not have been the best available development path.[^c4-6]

Suppose, in a thought experiment, a widely used software system has trained operators, tested connectors, and a large collection of fixes. A rival's underlying design might be promising, yet the established system can be the practical choice today. Its accumulated advantages are services users actually value. The argument for examining its history does not require pretending those services vanish when we recognize how they developed.

Could another history have developed a better set of capabilities? Changing a price at the end may not recreate the development that never happened along the way. A later subsidy can alter incentives; it does not automatically supply years of missing experience.

Bell's technology sharing makes the constructive side visible. Accumulated knowledge can become an input for other people's work. A strong incumbent need not keep every improvement inside its own boundary. An institution may preserve the benefits of a developed technology while allowing others to learn from and extend it. Whether a particular rule achieves that depends on its practical terms.

Historical dependence, increasing returns, and technological dominance do not by themselves establish a finite lifetime comparison budget. To investigate that property, we need the allocation probabilities and an account of how productive inputs affect them.

### The machine and the workforce

The keyboard offers a familiar version of the problem, and a warning about telling it too confidently. Paul David's 1985 paper used QWERTY to explain historical dependence through the interaction of machines, trained typists, and conversion costs. S. J. Liebowitz and Stephen Margolis subsequently disputed his empirical account, including the evidence offered for the advantages of switching. The keyboard is a contested case, not a settled demonstration of an inferior standard trapping the economy.[^c4-7]

The underlying coordination question remains useful. Imagine an employer choosing equipment that existing workers already know how to use. Choosing it can be sensible even if a different arrangement might have worked well had training developed differently. To establish that switching would improve matters now, we would need evidence about both the alternative's performance and the costs of getting there. Pointing to a different imaginable beginning would not settle the decision.

Transition costs change what a comparison measures. A technology can perform well in a demonstration while slowing work during training. An afternoon's performance and a year's performance may answer different questions. The evaluation horizon must follow the decision, rather than be selected afterward to favor a preferred technology.

Hush-A-Phone sharpens the contrast. Its case did not require proving that every subscriber should use the attachment. A subscriber's reasonable choice about privacy was narrower than a claim that the whole network should be reorganized around the device. The scope of the requested opportunity mattered. Requiring proof of a total technological revolution before allowing a modest use would have set a peculiar standard for entry.

For an investigator of historical advantage, these examples impose a useful discipline. Identify the change being evaluated. Describe the cost of adapting to it. Look for evidence capable of distinguishing a promising alternative from an appealing story. That discipline protects the case for useful comparisons: its credibility should not depend on every famous account of lock-in turning out to be correct.

### Finding out what nobody knew

Friedrich Hayek put discovery at the center of his account of competition. In a lecture delivered in 1968, published in the English translation cited here in 2002, he argued that competition reveals facts that would otherwise remain unknown or unused. Its point involves uncertainty about the particular results it will produce. If every relevant answer were already known, the discovery process would have little to discover.[^c4-8]

One outside learner was Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo, later renamed Sony. Its corporate history describes engineers studying a technical book and reports Kazuo Iwama sent from a 1954 visit to Western Electric. The team had to develop its own manufacturing equipment. Acquiring rights had not delivered a finished factory.[^c4-sony]

Discovering a useful product and measuring each input's contribution to its maker's rewards are different tasks. A market can show that something works while leaving us uncertain about how its producer became the one able to offer it.

Licensing had not delivered a saleable radio. Sony's account describes a prototype, the TR-52, whose housing warped as the weather warmed in 1955. Plans to sell it were abandoned; the redesigned TR-55 reached the market later that year. A working transistor had left a practical problem unresolved.[^c4-sony]

The distinction is visible in the objects: a licensed invention, manufacturing equipment, a failed casing, a redesigned product. An opportunity to develop can generate problems nobody has yet priced into a finished offer. Solving them takes further work.

At Hush-A-Phone's scale, a user could learn whether the privacy benefit justified the effect on a conversation. At a laboratory's scale, a company could learn how to turn a semiconductor technique into a product. These are different forms of discovery. Preserving the opportunity for them does not require a planner who already knows all the answers. It requires rules under which relevant answers can be produced and used.

There is no reason to expect that process to be costless or universally successful. A pilot can teach the buyer that a product is unsuitable. A development project can consume resources without producing an improvement. Competition's capacity to discover includes discovering disappointments. The economic justification for an opportunity concerns the value of what might be learned, assessed before knowing which result will arrive.

### Who gets to accumulate a record

Merton's account of cumulative advantage in science connects recognition to the resources and attention that support later work. The advantage can change what a researcher is able to do next. It need not consist solely of an audience mistaking an unchanged piece of work for a better one. This is why a serious explanation of unequal success has to allow capability itself to develop along the way.[^c4-9]

Opportunity can produce the evidence used to allocate the next opportunity. Paid work finances development, exposes practical problems, and establishes a record. A weaker later application can therefore be both a consequence of earlier exclusion and a reason for another rejection.

The field experiments discussed in Chapter 1 isolated one part of this sequence. Van de Rijt and colleagues randomly supplied initial advantages and observed subsequent success in several settings. Their Kickstarter experiment found more later third-party support for projects given an initial donation. Larger initial advantages did not produce proportionate increases without limit. The causal evidence concerned specified interventions, rather than a universal law of runaway success.[^c4-10]

Lacking a record differs from being unable to acquire a useful one. The first is an observation; the second concerns a development process. Treating them as interchangeable allows an existing allocation history to justify its own continuation.

Bell's licensing change can be understood as altering one input into such processes. A firm with access to a useful invention could attempt work previously made more difficult by licensing terms. The work would then generate its own results. This interpretation places the opportunity between existing knowledge and later achievement. It does not assume that removing one obstacle removes all the others.

Alchian's account of profit-based selection supplies another established perspective. A process can select surviving firms without requiring every firm to foresee or deliberately optimize the circumstances that favor it. In the Bell setting, the relevant environment includes the rules for using knowledge and reaching customers. Surviving within that environment and identifying the contribution of each input remain different analytical tasks.[^c4-11]

Selection and institutional analysis belong together. Success can reflect skillful adaptation to existing requirements. Whether those requirements were useful, whether alternatives could have developed under different arrangements, and what future changes would accomplish are additional questions.

### Discovery inside the large organization

Bell's own laboratory supplies the other side. Bardeen described a field-effect proposal that initially failed. Investigating the failure directed research toward semiconductor surfaces; the point-contact transistor emerged from that program. The large organization could pursue an unpromising result until it opened another route. Productive internal discovery belongs beside the outside opportunities in this history.[^c4-1]

A patent list would not have performed that investigation. It required materials, instruments, theory, and people able to work together. Breaking those capacities apart might have made the experiment harder. Their value helps explain why opening a boundary and dismantling an organization are different proposals. The telephone attachment could be assessed without pretending the laboratory supplied nothing worth preserving.

The 1952 symposium adds a second dimension. Knowledge developed inside a large organization could also be transmitted beyond it. Internal research and outside experimentation can be complements. The question is how the arrangement connects them: what is shared, which uses are feasible, what costs are covered, and whether someone outside can produce a result that the larger organization would not have produced itself.

The relevant information set remains crucial. Internal experiments that reveal the input effect under investigation must be counted. An outsider's inability to inspect those experiments raises questions of access and verification; it does not establish that the evidence was never produced. The next chapter examines that distinction.

Likewise, an outside supplier is not automatically a fresh, independent history. It may use the same underlying components, draw on the same training, or depend on the same route to customers. These connections can be efficient. They also affect what a comparison can distinguish. Counting company names without examining the underlying experiment would give the institutional arrangement an informational meaning it has not yet earned.

Bell's record therefore supports a question more specific than whether large firms innovate. Which kinds of learning does a particular arrangement make easier? Which does it discourage or prevent? What could a feasible alternative preserve? Those questions recognize the laboratory's productive organization while leaving room to examine the restrictions around it.

### The company's own case

AT&T's annual report for 1955 described an experiment in Americus, Georgia. Equipment using transistors carried five conversations over a single pair of rural telephone wires. The report also described experimental use of a Bell solar battery to provide part of the equipment's power. Here was discovery inside the system, directed at a practical service problem.[^c4-14]

The report made the company's argument for integration explicit: the people designing equipment, manufacturing and installing it, and operating the service belonged to a unified organization. In discussing the decree, it welcomed the preservation of those relationships. It predicted that major services would continue and that revenues and earnings would not be appreciably affected. Those were the company's contemporary claims and expectations, rather than a later independent evaluation.[^c4-14]

The Americus example gives the argument something concrete to rest on. Coordinating a new component, its power supply, and an operating service can require several kinds of expertise. The case for an integrated organization should therefore be examined as a claim about how work gets done. Dismissing it merely because the organization benefits from integration would leave the productive mechanism unexplored.

At the same time, successful coordination inside an organization cannot establish that every outside restriction is necessary to achieve it. A test of rural equipment and a rule barring a telephone attachment concern different activities. Evidence that the first worked does not settle the second. Following the actual activity keeps a broad institutional defense from standing in for the narrower comparison a particular decision needs.

Integration and specialization create empirical tradeoffs. An interface may introduce failures an integrated service avoids, or permit a component to improve without replacing the whole system. The consequences depend on the technical boundary and must be tested.

Requiring proof that an entire replacement system works before permitting a component test can make that test prohibitively costly. Such a requirement needs a technical or economic justification, particularly when the component can be evaluated safely on its own.

There is a matching mistake in treating the component's success as proof that the whole system could be replaced cheaply. A successful limited pilot can supply useful evidence about that pilot. It may say little about coordination at a larger scale. Advocates of access need to respect this boundary as much as advocates of integration need to respect the limits of evidence for their own arrangement.

The company's report therefore belongs beside the patent study and the attachment cases. Each speaks to a different question. A claim about productive coordination, evidence about follow-on invention, and a judgment about a particular restriction should be allowed to inform one another. They should not be collapsed into a vote on whether the entire institution deserves praise or blame.

A bounded interface can preserve much of an integrated service while allowing a relevant component comparison. Reliability, coordination costs, and productive gains determine whether that arrangement is worthwhile.

### A rule for connecting

In 1968, the FCC addressed another independently supplied device: the Carterfone, invented by Thomas Carter. It connected a private two-way radio system with the telephone network. The telephone handset rested in a cradle; the arrangement allowed a radio user and a telephone caller to speak through the base station. This was a practical connection between systems, rather than a new public telephone network.[^c4-12]

The telephone companies treated its use as prohibited under their tariff. The FCC found that the Carterfone met a need and did not adversely affect the telephone system. Its decision rejected a blanket prohibition covering harmless as well as harmful interconnecting devices. The Commission preserved the possibility of reasonable standards and restrictions on devices that actually caused harm.[^c4-12]

The cradle makes the economic issue visible. A person could possess the radio, the telephone, and a device capable of joining the conversation, yet face a rule forbidding that combination. The performance of the combination could not be inferred from a telephone's success on its own. A new use required an opportunity at the boundary between established systems.

The FCC also allowed the network to change. Telephone companies could improve their systems and revise reasonable standards. Independent manufacturers would have to comply; an owner might have to rebuild a device or stop using it. Permission to connect did not oblige the network to preserve every old attachment forever. It created a boundary with responsibilities on both sides.[^c4-12]

That rule changes the entrant's task. Instead of seeking permission simply because its equipment came from elsewhere, it must satisfy protective requirements. Those requirements can themselves be disputed and evaluated. The economic promise is a workable route to use, with a test of the claimed harm. Whether a particular implementation delivers that promise remains an empirical question.

Notice how the institutional question has changed since the patent opening. Permission to use an invention addresses one barrier. Permission to attach a device addresses another. A technical standard can address a third, while a customer's willingness to pay remains a fourth. A policy directed at one stage should be judged against what happens along the whole route, including the obstacles that remain afterward.

A right to connect becomes useful when someone can actually make the connection. The device must work with the network, and the terms must allow it to be used. Hush-A-Phone and Carterfone let us follow the gap between legal permission and practical opportunity.

### Opening a route, paying for it

An experiment useful for future decisions can still be expensive for present users. Staff time, duplicate infrastructure, migration, and temporary reliability losses belong in the cost of preserving an opportunity.

There are costs on the innovation side as well. A policy that makes existing knowledge easier to use can affect the rewards expected from producing knowledge in the first place. That is a prospective incentive question. It requires attention to how research is financed and how the rule changes behavior. The usefulness of follow-on work does not remove it from the evaluation.

Bell's licensing intervention produced different innovation outcomes inside and outside telecommunications. A proposed policy in another industry needs to identify the knowledge at stake, the decisions it could improve, and the costs of preserving or replacing it.

The benefit is the improvement in decisions supported by additional evidence. An independent evaluation or better logging may answer the question more cheaply; an adequate result may already exist. Some uncertainty will be too costly to resolve.

Timing can alter those costs. Once infrastructure is organized around a single approach, an alternative can become expensive to test. Preserving a limited route earlier may retain an option difficult to recreate later.

The proposal remains conditional. Early pilots can be badly chosen, poorly measured, or retained after they stop helping. An institution committed to learning should be able to close an unproductive program as well as open a useful one. Preserving comparisons is a means to better decisions. It is not an instruction to maintain every experiment indefinitely or to declare every participant entitled to continued support.

### The histories inside the system

Bell's transistor, its technology-sharing program, the patent decree, and the attachment disputes belong in one account because they concern successive stages of economic possibility. Research produced something new. Licensing affected who could build on it. Rules about use affected who could reach an actual customer. At each stage, productive organization and institutional permission helped shape what happened next.

The resulting history contains real achievements. It also leaves questions about routes that were harder to pursue. We can investigate those questions through actual interventions and comparisons, as the patent study did. We can use shadow futures to state what remains unobserved. We should keep the evidence for an intervention distinct from the imagined histories that help formulate the attribution problem.

The cup and the cradle bring us back to the carpenter and the two towns. A boundary can let an independent invention reach an established network. It can also make the network owner the judge of what everyone connected to it may try. Digital markets extend that question: how many apparent destinations still lead to the same decision about who deserves a chance?

New technologies need feasible routes to development, delivery, and evaluation. Accomplishments deserve to be understood within the arrangements that produced them, including the rules governing who can attempt the next one. The histories that become infrastructure matter because later possibilities must pass through them.
