# Chapter 11

## Taxing lost comparison

Sweden introduced its nitrogen-oxide charge in 1992. Covered energy-production facilities paid for emissions, while receipts were returned in relation to useful energy output. The design separated a harmful by-product from the productive activity that generated it.[^c11-5]

A business decision can improve current production while closing a route to discovering alternatives. If that closure prevents useful learning that others cannot replace, its private return omits a cost borne elsewhere. Making the decision maker face that cost changes the incentive to close the route. A corrective charge is one instrument. An access obligation may preserve the comparison more directly.

There are two tax questions here. One asks how confidently income can be divided between productive contribution and historical position. The other asks whether particular conduct imposes an external cost by preventing valuable comparison. Explaining a fortune and changing future incentives are different tasks.

## Income and its explanation

Payroll records, completed work, and measured output establish meaningful facts. They do not necessarily identify how much an input changed the probability of receiving a reward. A causal question about revenue also differs from one about a product's benefit to users. Each requires its own target, observations, and comparison.

Consider the fixed-input version of the allocation model. An admissible adjustment to hidden position can offset a change in the productive-input effect, leaving every observable allocation probability unchanged. Two candidate explanations can then assign the same law to all recorded histories while giving different answers about what changing the input would do. A rule based solely on that record has no observation on which to distinguish the exact contribution divisions.

This is an observational-equivalence argument. The underlying effects differ between candidate explanations; a shadow replay instead preserves those effects and varies the realized history. It is also separate from the finite-lifetime-comparison result. Input variation that the hidden position cannot absorb, or additional informative outcomes, may break the equivalence. The limitation concerns the specified experiment, rather than every possible form of economic evidence.

Taxation can proceed without an exact retrospective causal audit of every reward. Thomas Piketty, Emmanuel Saez, and Stefanie Stantcheva distinguish responses involving productive activity, avoidance, and compensation bargaining. The different behaviors have different implications for tax policy.[^c11-3]

Casey Rothschild and Florian Scheuer analyze taxation when income combines productive and rent-seeking activities that the authority cannot separately observe for each person. Changing a tax can alter relative returns across activities, strengthening or weakening a direct correction. Averaging the hidden components does not automatically produce the right rate.[^c11-4]

These approaches assess consequences using assumptions about behavior and welfare. They leave room for observable bases and plausible ranges without requiring exact knowledge of individual merit. The comparison-budget argument challenges a stronger confidence in what a reward record must eventually reveal; it does not make public finance impossible.

Moral desert and contractual entitlement require further judgments. A valid promise of payment can matter while its recipient's complete causal contribution remains uncertain. Security against income loss, rewards for valuable investment, and practical routes into new work pursue different aims. Evidence about alternative histories does not turn them into one policy or dictate equal incomes.

## What would a charge measure?

The Swedish charge depends on a verification chain. The environmental agency describes instruments, declarations, and audits. Facility equipment must be compared at least annually with independent measurements from accredited laboratories, and the agency coordinates checks of the laboratories themselves.[^c11-5]

Lost comparison has no corresponding physical meter. A prevented assignment could have revealed a serious failure, repeated something already known, or produced an unreliable measurement. Its value depends on the decision it could change and the evidence available elsewhere. Counting missing assignments cannot establish those differences.

The comparison budget accumulates modeled allocation chance outside the current favorite. To move from that statistical object to money requires an account of how conduct changes opportunities, what observations those opportunities produce, and what their absence costs. A finite budget supplies no monetary damage estimate by itself.

Removing a restriction may leave an experiment infeasible if participants still lack resources, technical access, or a valid outcome measure. Another source may already supply the evidence. The loss caused by the restriction depends on what a feasible alternative would have made possible.

Scale can make replacement harder. Losing one local route may leave independent institutions elsewhere. Losing access through a gate shared across a large network can remove many practical routes together. That is a hypothesis about institutional dependence to investigate, alongside the productive gains the shared system supplies.

Beneficiaries matter too. A firm already has an incentive to buy information whose benefits it captures. Other users can benefit from an investigation without paying its producer. Kenneth Arrow examined the difficulty of appropriating the returns to information, whether generated deliberately or as a by-product of ordinary activity.[^c11-6] This established information problem supplies a foundation for asking what useful evidence an exclusionary action prevents.

The proposed correction must count both sides. Productive integration can reduce operating costs, while secure evaluation may preserve a useful test without unrestricted disclosure. Privacy, security, disruption, and the cost of maintaining access belong in the comparison. The economic objective is a better attainable arrangement, with all its resource requirements included.

## Production and private testing

A firm's response can occur on more than one margin. It can change a restrictive activity and change how much evaluation it purchases. Private testing is real evidence. If it supplies the same usable information to the same decision makers in time, treating the original loss as wholly unreplaced overstates that loss.

Equivalence needs to be demonstrated. An internal AI benchmark can use synthetic tasks while a field study observes real users; their errors may overlap, and their results may reach different people. Internal findings unavailable to an affected outside decision maker cannot automatically replace that person's missing evidence.

In their model of information acquisition, Luca Colombo, Gianluca Femminis, and Alessandro Pavan show that privately chosen information can differ from the socially efficient amount. Depending on the setting, the discrepancy can run in either direction.[^c11-7] Underinvestment therefore needs a specific account of benefits that private incentives insufficiently protect.

Different instruments affect those incentives differently. A charge on a restrictive term changes the return to imposing it. Payment for verified evaluation changes the return to producing evidence. Opening access may reduce the need for purchased tests; subsidizing tests may finance work that would have occurred anyway. Those interactions determine what a correction achieves.

Convenient tax bases can combine unlike activities. Computing produces ordinary outputs and also supports experiments investigating failures. A charge on all computing reaches both. Electricity, emissions, revenue, and distribution can justify separate tax questions, but an information-based correction needs evidence connecting its chosen base to the relevant loss.

Profit is no more direct a meter. Useful engineering, scarcity, historical advantage, and restrictive terms can all contribute to earnings. A corrective proposal must identify conduct and consequences, rather than use the size of a return as a substitute for them.

## When public information displaces private effort

Colombo, Femminis, and Pavan also establish that greater public information precision reduces privately acquired precision in their specified model.[^c11-8] The result concerns an endogenous response, rather than a universal one-for-one cancellation of tests. Evaluating public provision requires counting what private participants change afterward.

A public result equivalent to one a firm planned to purchase can alter who pays without adding evidence. It may still save resources if public provision is cheaper. Different public measurements can instead expose failures private testing missed, reach other recipients, or supply work beyond private capacity. Announced test counts cannot distinguish those outcomes.

California's Electric Program Investment Charge provides a documented way to finance shared investigation. Utility customers fund research administered by the Energy Commission and participating utilities, supporting electricity-system research, development, and demonstration.[^c11-9]

During the December 20, 2022 Ferndale earthquake, the Redwood Coast Airport Microgrid supplied nearly fifteen hours of backup during a regional outage lasting nearly sixteen hours, according to the project team's January 2026 report. Customers included an airport and a Coast Guard air station.[^c11-10]

The installation also had a persistent fault. Converters connecting the large solar array to the batteries repeatedly triggered protective trips. The report identified an unwanted current path to ground, particularly in humid conditions. Replacement equipment was eventually installed and tested. Another part of the system allowed backup operation despite the solar-converter problem.[^c11-10]

A demonstration can therefore provide service, expose a costly failure, and potentially inform later projects. These benefits have different recipients. The team found that readily monetizable benefits did not cover assessed costs, while the value of resilience depended on the community and services involved.[^c11-10] Establishing a worthwhile subsidy requires assessing wider benefits against the full costs, including what private activity it replaces.

Knowledge transmission is another step. A published diagnosis can help others only if it reaches them in a usable form and applies sufficiently to their problem. AI evaluation faces the same requirement: findings must be interpretable, relevant to subsequent tasks, and capable of changing a consequential decision. A large collection of scores can be less useful than a small test that resolves an important uncertainty.

William Kerr, Ramana Nanda, and Matthew Rhodes-Kropf describe entrepreneurship as experimentation, with staged commitments allowing learning before full investment.[^c11-11] A negative result can save resources by supporting abandonment. Its value comes from what was credibly learned, rather than failure itself.

## Replacement and the feasible alternative

David Blackwell compared experiments through the decisions their information permits. If one can reproduce another's observations through a known conversion rule independent of the hidden state, a decision maker can imitate the second experiment's choices.[^c11-12] This supplies a discipline for evaluating whether evidence is an adequate substitute; acquisition and implementation costs require an additional calculation.

A lost field comparison may be replaceable by a cheaper independent evaluation. That route can reduce the sacrifice while preserving useful information. It must reach the relevant recipients in time, fit the decision, and account for capacity and delivery costs. A laboratory measurement that omits the development and learning enabled by real participation may not supply the same opportunity.

Replacement also changes the institution. A shadow replay preserves starting conditions and response rules; a new laboratory or access program changes them. The evidence such an intervention produces can be valuable without being equivalent to the lost history.

Secure infrastructure, qualified staff, independent checking, and documentation create fixed costs before the first result appears. Existing evaluators or shared facilities can reduce duplication, while common errors and centralized control can create new dependencies. A limited commission may establish feasibility before permanent provision is justified.

These considerations favor comparing whole arrangements: preserving access, buying private evaluations, supplying shared tests, or combining narrower measures. Useful institutional invention can retain the benefits of scale while reopening a consequential comparison. Preserving every old channel indefinitely is no substitute for that appraisal.

## Financing and incentives

Britain's 2017 retail-banking order required providers to support implementation arrangements and fund independent service-quality surveys, with contributions linked to active accounts in the relevant survey market.[^c11-13] The account rule allocates a bill for specified duties. It does not measure each provider's responsibility for lost evidence.

Financing useful evaluation and correcting harmful conduct are different achievements. General revenue, supplier contributions, and charges tied to a demonstrated restrictive action produce different incentives. Raising enough money for a laboratory does not establish that the funding instrument targets the cause of the information loss.

The payment is also different from the resources consumed. A transfer can finance staff time, computing, and supervision; counting both the transfer and those inputs as destroyed output would double-count resource cost. Who pays still matters for distribution and behavior, including changes in prices, wages, investment, and entry.

Public provision has an opportunity cost as well. Money and expertise used for evaluation may displace another useful activity. Even a good laboratory can be financed through a charge that discourages valuable production or testing. Its funding and corrective effects require separate examination.

Verification is part of that expense. Repetitive easy tasks can inflate a test count, and selective cases can flatter an entrant. Relevant task selection, checked outcomes, version records, and independent scrutiny help establish what evidence was actually produced. A nominally cheap substitute can be costly once those safeguards are included.

## Who pays for the next attempt?

Suppose a dominant AI supplier controls the only practical interface through which an available rival could be evaluated. If a restrictive term prevents a useful test, the first question is whether an access duty can restore it. A charge that leaves the rival locked out may raise money without fixing the obstruction. Where a bounded, secure arrangement can restore the route at a justified cost, opening it addresses the problem directly.

Where conduct still imposes a measurable external loss, a corrective charge should reflect that loss after feasible replacement and private responses are counted. Revenues could support independent evaluation or access. Spending the money well is a separate obligation: the public laboratory has to produce usable evidence, and affected people must be able to act on it. The firm's wealth alone supplies no estimate of the damage.

Now consider a team that could use the interface but cannot finance the work needed to reach a credible trial. Access rules alone leave it outside. Shared research and wider access to productive assets require financing, and relying entirely on existing owners lets their willingness to fund a challenge decide which challenges occur. Progressive taxation of capital income and large inheritances can supply part of that financing and spread ownership beyond its current holders. Its justification is the productive participation and independent discovery it makes possible. No verdict about each taxpayer's moral desert is needed.

Florian Scheuer and Joel Slemrod's review of wealth taxation examines the choice of tax base, valuation, avoidance, enforcement, and behavioral responses. Those choices change what a tax collects and how people respond.[^c11-wealth] A tax on annual wealth, a tax on capital income, and an inheritance tax reach different things. Workable capital income and inheritance taxation offer routes to financing broader participation; their investment and financing effects determine the terms. Uncertainty about contribution alone supplies neither a preferred instrument nor a tax rate.

The uses of the revenue should be concrete. A shared computing facility could let a qualified team attempt a different model. An independently governed laboratory could test failures that no supplier has an incentive to disclose. Grants could finance credible first demonstrations. Broadly held funds or employee ownership could distribute claims on productive assets beyond their existing owners. These are proposals for widening the means to attempt and benefit from economic activity; their performance has to be measured against their costs.

A payment to a household can make it easier to survive a failed attempt or to acquire training. It can also serve the straightforward purpose of protecting income. It need not generate a comparison to be worthwhile. But a policy advertised as restoring contestability has to reach the missing resource or permission: cash cannot, by itself, open a closed interface.

The public funder should publish qualification rules, allow competing methods, and provide appeal against exclusion. A grant recipient should be able to report failure without concealing it to protect the program. Continuing support should depend on what is learned and on the credible value of further work. Public finance should create alternatives to private gatekeeping while remaining answerable to independent judgment itself.

These fiscal purposes should remain visible in the accounts. A conduct-based charge seeks to change a harmful incentive. General taxation finances shared capabilities and broader participation. Pooling their revenues may be convenient, but financing a valuable laboratory does not establish that every contributor caused an equivalent informational loss. Nor should the same harm be charged twice through overlapping instruments.

Capital can finance the equipment, time, and risk that discovery requires. It can also accumulate around a route whose owner decides who else may enter. Capital income and large inheritances should help finance the shared capabilities through which other people can become productive owners. The terms must preserve reasons to invest. Leaving accumulated returns entirely outside that responsibility allows a successful position to pass to the next generation together with the power to prevent its challengers from developing.

## Choosing rules before knowing every contribution

A defensible charge needs a causal chain: identified conduct, changed comparative evidence, affected decisions and beneficiaries, and an assessed loss after private responses and feasible replacement. It must then improve the attainable outcome after productive benefits, financing, enforcement, and entry are counted.

The time horizon must match on both sides. Software changes can make some findings obsolete while leaving enduring failure modes relevant. Longer effects on learning and entry require stated assumptions and evidence. A temporary information problem should not become a permanent fiscal category after its basis has changed.

Participation can change too. Qualification costs may deter useful entrants; predictable obligations may support investment. Warnings about reduced innovation need investigation, rather than automatic acceptance or dismissal. These are responses affecting the case for a particular instrument.

Access duties, payments for verified testing, public provision, and existing private arrangements have to be compared on those terms. Where the evidence cannot support a precise corrective rate, a narrower access remedy or a limited trial can establish what works. General financing choices can still proceed through an explicit public judgment about benefits, burdens, and incentives.

We do not need agreement about the exact share of every fortune attributable to its owner before choosing a tax system. We need agreement about what that system should make possible, how its costs will be borne, and how its results can be challenged. A reward record that cannot settle contribution should not be asked to certify the rules protecting its own distribution.

A settlement that rewards useful investment, broadens ownership, and finances independent routes to discovery gives enterprise a future beyond its current owners. A successful business can earn substantial returns while contributing to the resources through which another business might demonstrate a better way. The next task is to make those opportunities observable in the institutions economics studies.
