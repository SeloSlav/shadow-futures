# Introduction

## The evidence success leaves behind

### Why I wrote this book

The question that brought me to this book is how a market can become better at producing things while losing the evidence needed to explain the rewards it distributes.

I studied economics at the University of Victoria in Canada and worked in door-to-door sales, landscaping, and software development. I have worked on cloud infrastructure and deployments and tried to build an olive-oil business. Those experiences kept bringing me back to the distance between doing useful work, persuading someone to try it, and receiving a reward. A good product still needs a route to a customer who can recognize its quality. Working software has to reach the systems and people who can use it.

Melanie Mitchell, Douglas Hofstadter, and M. Mitchell Waldrop drew me into complexity through their popular writing. I went on to read W. Brian Arthur, Stuart A. Kauffman, Eric Beinhocker, John Holland, and Kenneth Arrow, and traveled to Washington, D.C., for a Santa Fe Institute short course. Arthur's increasing returns offered a way to think about advantages that grow through use.[^i6] Kauffman's adjacent possible directed my attention toward the possibilities an existing world puts within reach.[^i12] I wanted to understand what happens to alternatives that never get the opportunity to develop.

In September 2016, I published a Medium essay called “Increasing Returns and the New World of Hindsight.” I was already asking how a company's growth could strengthen its confidence in a history that left its alternatives unseen.[^i19] The comparison budget came later. The question had been following me for years.

I developed this question in a research paper, *Shadow Futures: Contribution Uncertainty and the Self-Reinforcing Market*, available on SSRN. It introduces the comparison budget and shows how opportunities outside the favorite constrain what an allocation record can reveal about productive contribution.[^i2]

This book follows that question through experiments, economic history, and the emerging AI economy. I want to show what we can learn about productive work, where the evidence runs out, and how we can preserve opportunities to discover something different. An advertisement credited with a sale offers a useful place to begin.

### The advertisement that got the credit

A customer searches for eBay, clicks an advertisement for eBay, and buys something on eBay. For the advertiser, the journey looks reassuringly complete. There was an ad, then a click, then a sale. Each step can be recorded. Money went out to attract the customer; money came back when the customer bought. Here, apparently, is an unusually clean account of a contribution.

Except that the customer was searching for eBay already.

Economists Thomas Blake, Chris Nosko, and Steven Tadelis studied large advertising experiments at the company. When advertising against its own brand terms was suspended, customers largely substituted unpaid search links for paid ones. The brand advertisements had no measurable short-term benefit. Experiments with more general search terms found a different pattern: new and infrequent customers could be influenced, while frequent customers accounted for much spending without a corresponding change in purchases.[^i1]

One brand-keyword test stopped eBay advertising on Yahoo and Microsoft's search platform while retaining Google advertising as a comparison. Almost all the lost paid-search traffic and attributed sales reappeared through unpaid links. Customers had another route to the shop. Closing the paid route revealed how many sales the advertising ledger had mistakenly credited.[^i1]

The advertisements existed. People designed them, placed them, and paid for them. Some helped customers. Counting that activity did not establish how much it changed purchases.

For the shopper who wanted eBay, the alternative was close at hand: another link on a search page. In other settings, the alternative is much harder to recover. It might be another business that never attracted a first customer, another worker who never received an assignment, or another technology that never developed a record of successful use. We can study what happened in great detail while remaining uncertain about what those alternatives would have revealed.

Success can close the comparisons needed to explain success. An early reward changes who gets the next serious opportunity, and therefore what later observers can learn. The economy produces goods and services while selecting which alternatives acquire a history. More transactions can deepen the record of a winner without settling how much its productive work caused the lead.

To measure the advertisement's short-term contribution, eBay needed customers who could arrive without it. Recording the paid clicks more accurately would not recreate that comparison.

### How success changes the evidence

Economists and sociologists have long studied how success builds on itself. Armen Alchian explained how profits select surviving firms without requiring perfect foresight. Robert Merton described how recognition brings scientists resources for further work. W. Brian Arthur showed how increasing returns can let early adoption influence which technology becomes established.[^i6]

Network science offers another familiar mechanism. In preferential attachment, a web page that already has many links is more likely to attract another link. Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert combined that process with network growth to explain a power-law distribution of links, with a small number of highly connected hubs.[^i13] Growth, lower costs, and unequal outcomes can accompany either plentiful comparisons or very few. Explaining a winner's contribution requires evidence about the opportunities that actually remain to test alternatives.

Consider a platform that sends more tasks to workers with longer records. The feedback can widen their lead and get useful work done. It also determines what anyone can learn about workers who rarely receive a task. A thousand completed jobs for the favorite might reveal little about someone never hired. The problem concerns the production of evidence, before any judgment about whether the winner deserves the income.

### The work and the difference it made

I use contribution uncertainty for uncertainty about the effect of productive inputs on rewards in a specified economic setting. The inputs might include effort, quality, investment, or risk-bearing. The question concerns how an outcome would change if an input changed, while the relevant economic rules continued to apply. A result can be valuable, and the work behind it entirely real, while the size of that effect remains difficult to recover.[^i2]

Visits, purchases, and longer-term customer relationships are different outcomes. Contribution concerns a specified outcome, comparison, and time horizon.

Lockdown's two rankings raise a related question with a different design. MusicLab preserved independently developing markets for the same songs. The song's first-place finish and its fortieth-place finish were both outcomes of listeners choosing. They tell us that one observed ranking cannot be treated as a complete account of what that recording was capable of achieving. They do not calculate the value of every rehearsal or assign a percentage of the song's success to talent.[^i3]

A recording can give pleasure in several possible histories and receive very different amounts of attention in each. The pleasure is real. How much of the reward it explains remains a question about causes.

In George Akerlof's account of the used-car market, the seller may know more about the car's quality than the buyer does. A buyer unable to distinguish good cars from bad ones may offer a price that drives good cars away. The information problem changes which trades take place.[^i4]

My starting question allows the inputs to be observed. Suppose the buyer can inspect the work, rather than suspecting a hidden defect. That still leaves the effect of those inputs on the outcome to be learned. Knowing how much an advertising campaign cost does not identify the sales it created. Knowing what a musician recorded does not reveal every audience the recording could have found. Better disclosure and better comparison can solve different problems.

Kickstarter's experimental first donations helped some projects attract subsequent backing. In an ordinary business, an early customer can also finance better engineering, more practice, and a stronger organization. The advantage becomes productive when it improves what later customers receive. Asking whether the business is good and asking how it became good are separate inquiries.[^i5]

### Who gets a chance

Each choice does two things: it allocates resources now and helps determine what the next observer will be able to inspect. An expanding record of success can therefore coexist with fewer opportunities to evaluate alternatives.

For each allocation, given the history so far, identify the candidate with the greatest chance of receiving the reward. Take the chance that someone else receives it instead. Accumulate those chances across the opportunities under study. I call this economic quantity the comparison budget. The favorite can change. The total concerns the chances offered to alternatives, rather than the number of times an outsider actually wins.[^i2]

A list of awards and rejections does not, by itself, tell us those chances. The budget is not a count of winners or the number of firms on a website. To apply the idea empirically, we need evidence about the allocation process: which alternatives are eligible, what changes their prospects, and what is recorded beyond the award. The quantities cannot be read straight from market shares.

MusicLab helps us see why a catalogue is insufficient. A title remaining on a list does not mean listeners encounter it on comparable terms. Conversely, a single business can arrange useful tests of alternatives, as eBay did with advertising. Counting organizations and counting informative opportunities are different inquiries. Institutional design can matter as much as the number of logos on the screen.

Line Chloé Le Goff and Philippe Soulier had already connected reinforcement to estimation failure in urn models.[^i14] The comparison budget connects the remaining allocation chances to a limit on information about productive input effects. In my models it bounds how much information the reward record supplies. Under the additional conditions developed in Chapter 3, a finite lifetime budget prevents any method from reliably recovering contribution across every explanation the model allows. Independent measurements change the available evidence.[^i2]

A tiny but persistent chance of selecting an outsider can keep accumulating. A shrinking chance can also accumulate without limit if it shrinks slowly enough. Even then, comparisons must distinguish the input effects we care about. A trial that never varies the relevant engineering tells us little about its contribution, however often it runs.

Another transaction can demonstrate that an established service continues to work well while revealing little about an untested alternative. Commercial value and information about a particular comparison are separate properties of the same event.

Field experiments, independent technical evaluations, and measured outcomes can distinguish contributions that an award record leaves uncertain. Their information adds to what can be learned.

eBay could change where advertising appeared and observe what followed. Its advertising ledger did not have to remain the last word. A feasible intervention could supply the comparison that the existing record lacked.

### Other possible histories

I have spent an unreasonable amount of time thinking about the lives I did not live. A business begun earlier or a career pursued more consistently: hindsight makes it easy to furnish another life. It supplies the outcome I want and leaves out the decisions I would have faced along the way. The harder question concerns the possibilities that never became available to anyone.

The seed of this book's name was a phrase in Steven Johnson's account of Stuart A. Kauffman's adjacent possible. Kauffman, author of *At Home in the Universe*, explored self-organization and complexity. The adjacent possible describes possibilities reachable from what already exists. Johnson called it “a kind of shadow future.” Existing materials and discoveries put some next steps within reach; taking them can make further combinations possible.[^i12]

Think of Bell's transistor. An invention can become a component in devices its inventors never built. Who can work with it matters to which possibilities are explored. Johnson's image made me ask an economic question: what happens to possibilities that depend on opportunities going to someone who never gets them?

I chose *shadow futures* because the phrase holds together two views of the same opportunity. Before a customer places an order, more than one business may have a future that could grow from it. Afterward, we can follow the business that won: the revenue it receives, the investment it makes, the work it learns to do. The other path recedes from view. It may never leave enough of a record for us to judge what could have developed there.

I use the term for feasible alternative allocation histories with the same starting conditions, underlying productive effects, and rules for how people respond. The first customer can change; so can the later investment financed by that first sale. The rules stay fixed while events unfold differently. Kauffman's adjacent possible concerns the opening of new possibilities. My question concerns what we can learn when only one of the available paths becomes observable.

MusicLab makes the idea visible. Lockdown could finish first or fortieth without becoming a different recording. The researchers kept the other chart. An ordinary business rarely gets an equivalent record of the opportunities it missed ten years ago.

That is why I chose a phrase about unrealized futures for a result about knowledge. A path we do not observe can contain a comparison we need to explain the one we do. Another order for the winner adds to its history. It need not tell us what the rival would have learned, built, or earned if an earlier order had gone the other way.

This book is itself a product of the adjacent possible. Those writers gave me questions I could carry into jobs and ventures they knew nothing about. Its future will depend on who encounters it and what they can do with it.

If someone else tests the idea more rigorously, explains it better, or helps more people use it, I will be content with that outcome. Credit matters, as does the freedom to challenge a claim. What I do not want is an epistemic monopoly over an idea that could benefit humanity. I want the comparison to remain open, including when an alternative to my account proves better. I cannot ask readers to take shadow futures seriously and then assume that the history giving me the most recognition must be the best one.

Changing an early award within unchanged rules explores another history. Changing the rules is an intervention. eBay altered an arrangement and measured the response. A simulated alteration would also need evidence that its programmed responses fit the actual market.

Predicting what comes next and explaining why the outcome has the shape it does answer different questions. In Lockdown's winning world, an observer who expects more listeners to choose an already popular song could make useful forecasts. Those forecasts would not reveal the other world's chart. Getting the next choice right does not itself account for the song's lead.

Blackwell and Dubins showed that, under specified conditions, observers' forecasts converge as evidence accumulates. Hao Zhang later demonstrated a statistical setting in which useful prediction coexists with failure to recover particular underlying parameters. Prediction and explanation can demand different things from the same record.[^i15]

An early research grant makes the stakes concrete. Thijs Bol, Mathijs de Vaan, and Arnout van de Rijt studied Dutch scientists whose applications fell just above or below a funding threshold. The near-winners subsequently accumulated more than twice as much further funding over eight years as the near-losers, excluding the initial grant itself. Part of the gap arose because unsuccessful applicants entered later funding competitions less often. The distinction involved who continued to present work for evaluation, as well as how evaluators treated it.[^i11]

The research proposal that never reaches another panel leaves a different kind of absence from the one a rejected proposal leaves. We can inspect the latter decision. The former requires asking which earlier events affected participation. When opportunity influences the decision to compete again, a record of later funding awards can conceal part of the process producing its own pool of candidates. That is a reason to follow the people outside the winner's history, even when the published results look complete.

### From evidence to institutions

At the customer-support desk studied by Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond, AI assistance helped workers resolve more issues per hour on average. The gains were particularly strong among less experienced and lower-skilled workers. Useful capability was reaching people who could benefit from it.[^i7]

Training future workers raises a further question. Today's assistant might help beginners learn, or do the tasks through which they would have learned. Comparing their later abilities requires following what they attempt, the guidance they receive, and what they can do afterward.

METR's developer research exposes one difficulty. As programmers became less willing to do certain work without AI, the research team found that participation and task selection made its follow-up estimates difficult to interpret. The work being studied was changing which comparisons people would supply. The team responded by reconsidering its design. Learning about the technology required attention to the institutions producing evidence about it.[^i8]

The reach of a judgment changes the stakes. Smith's artisan faced a market limited by transport; another town could offer another buyer. A platform can carry one rating across towns, while a shared infrastructure provider can control the route used by many apparently separate businesses. Physical limits persist, and old guilds also obstructed entry. What changes when a common gate spans the market is how far an exclusion travels.[^i16]

More computing can make experiments cheaper while routing more work through the same model, service, or evaluator. Where one institution controls the comparisons others need to judge alternatives, it holds an epistemic monopoly over those comparisons.[^i17] Productive scale and control over discovery can grow together.

Bell's 1956 settlement provides a historical warning against treating access as a slogan. Opening existing patents to royalty-free licensing was followed by greater innovation outside telecommunications. The study did not find the same result inside the sector, where barriers to reaching customers remained. A policy can loosen one constraint while leaving another in place. An entrant needs a usable route from permission to performance.[^i9]

Lost comparison can impose a cost that never appears on the customer's bill. A gatekeeper may improve its service while closing a route others need to develop or test theirs. When that closure deprives other people of valuable evidence, part of its cost falls outside the gatekeeper's decision. Many buyers can also favor the same route without one supplier controlling the whole market. Their transactions record the work completed; the missing trial leaves no failed product to count.

For such a market, waiting for a price increase can mean waiting until an alternative has lost its feasible beginning. Existing competition policy considers innovation and exclusion as well as prices.[^i18] Protecting a workable route to independent evaluation gives those concerns an earlier point of action. Credible threats of lasting closure should trigger a presumption for keeping that route open, with security, investment, and the costs of access included in its terms.

For taxation, explaining an income and changing future incentives are different tasks. Conduct that prevents useful comparison can impose costs on others, justifying a corrective charge where those costs are established. Shared research, broader ownership, and a universal basic income can give the public a stake in the returns while preserving the capacity to begin again. Their case need not depend on proving that particular recipients were morally undeserving.

Consider a capable team that cannot afford its first credible trial while existing owners use their returns to consolidate the routes to customers. Leaving both positions untouched is a choice about the next opportunity. Using part of capital income and large inheritances to finance shared research and broader access to assets is another. The second can support an economy with room for new judgments and new owners. Its tax rates and spending choices must be assessed through investment, avoidance, administration, and the benefits people actually receive.

An independent test, access to existing records, and a change in market rules produce different evidence at different costs. A useful remedy improves a consequential decision enough to justify those costs. Sometimes the evidence will favor keeping the existing arrangement.

### A different account of success

At Rothamsted, continuing agricultural experiments make the value of maintained comparison unusually tangible. Different treatments have been preserved while methods have also been adapted to keep the experiments relevant. The record matters because someone has continued the arrangements that produce it. Useful knowledge has a material history: plots, treatments, samples, labor, and decisions about which differences to keep.[^i10]

A first customer can finance a better product. A patent license can open a field of work. A distribution agreement can close it again. These decisions help determine whose ability gets demonstrated, how capital accumulates, and what competition policy and taxation can change.

A complete record of the eBay customer's paid click could have encouraged a confident story about the advertisement. Making another route observable changed the explanation. In more consequential settings, the missing route may be a business that never reaches a customer. Its absence from the record is precisely what makes it easy to overlook.
