# Introduction

## The evidence success leaves behind

### The advertisement that got the credit

A customer searches for eBay, clicks an advertisement for eBay, and buys something on eBay. For the advertiser, the journey looks reassuringly complete. There was an ad, then a click, then a sale. Each step can be recorded. Money went out to attract the customer; money came back when the customer bought. Here, apparently, is an unusually clean account of a contribution.

Except that the customer was searching for eBay already.

Economists Thomas Blake, Chris Nosko, and Steven Tadelis studied large advertising experiments at the company. When advertising against its own brand terms was suspended, customers largely substituted unpaid search links for paid ones. The brand advertisements had no measurable short-term benefit. Experiments with more general search terms found a different pattern: new and infrequent customers could be influenced, while frequent customers accounted for much spending without a corresponding change in purchases.[^i1]

One brand-keyword test stopped eBay advertising on Yahoo and Microsoft's search platform while retaining Google advertising as a comparison. Almost all the lost paid-search traffic and attributed sales reappeared through unpaid links. Customers had another route to the shop. Closing the paid route revealed how many sales the advertising ledger had mistakenly credited.[^i1]

This is a useful place to begin because it requires no quarrel about whether businesspeople work hard or whether customers know what they want. The advertisements existed. Someone designed them, arranged their placement, and paid for them. Some were useful to some customers. The difficult question was the difference they made to a specified outcome. Counting activity was an excellent way to establish activity. Establishing contribution required something more.

For the shopper who wanted eBay, the alternative was close at hand: another link on a search page. In other settings, the alternative is much harder to recover. It might be another business that never attracted a first customer, another worker who never received an assignment, or another technology that never developed a record of successful use. We can study what happened in great detail while remaining uncertain about what those alternatives would have revealed.

This book concerns that uncertainty, and a way markets themselves can help create it. An early reward can affect the opportunity to earn the next reward. When that happens, the market is also helping select the evidence that later observers will use to explain its success. The economy we inhabit is partly a system for producing things and partly a system for deciding which possibilities become observable.

Go back to the eBay search page. To discover the advertisement's short-term contribution, the researchers needed customers who could arrive without it. A manager looking only at the completed sequence of paid clicks and purchases would face a different task. More accurate recording of that sequence would not recreate the missing comparison. The distinction will matter whenever we hear that enough data should eventually explain everything.

### The work and the difference it made

I use contribution uncertainty for uncertainty about the effect of productive inputs on rewards in a specified economic setting. The inputs might include effort, quality, investment, or risk-bearing. The question concerns how an outcome would change if an input changed, while the relevant economic rules continued to apply. A result can be valuable, and the work behind it entirely real, while the size of that effect remains difficult to recover.[^i2]

The advertising example also reveals why the outcome must be specified. Increasing visits to a website is different from increasing purchases. Attracting a new customer is different from giving an existing customer a more convenient route. A short-term experiment may leave longer-term effects unresolved. Before asking how much something contributed, we need to say what it contributed to, compared with what, and over which period.

Lockdown's two rankings raise a related question with a different design. MusicLab preserved independently developing markets for the same songs. The song's first-place finish and its fortieth-place finish were both outcomes of listeners choosing. They tell us that one observed ranking cannot be treated as a complete account of what that recording was capable of achieving. They do not calculate the value of every rehearsal or assign a percentage of the song's success to talent.[^i3]

It is easy to move too quickly between those claims. Once we learn that success depends partly on a history, we may want to declare the winner undeserving. Once we notice that a winner made something useful, we may want to declare the reward fully explained. Neither conclusion follows. A recording can give pleasure in several possible histories, and the amount of attention it receives can differ greatly between them.

Consider another familiar difficulty. In George Akerlof's account of the used-car market, the seller may know more about the car's quality than the buyer does. A buyer unable to distinguish good cars from bad ones may offer a price that drives good cars away. The information problem changes which trades take place. Akerlof showed why uncertainty about quality belongs inside an account of how a market works.[^i4]

My starting question allows the inputs to be observed. Suppose the buyer can inspect the work, rather than suspecting a hidden defect. That still leaves the effect of those inputs on the outcome to be learned. Knowing how much an advertising campaign cost does not identify the sales it created. Knowing what a musician recorded does not reveal every audience the recording could have found. Better disclosure and better comparison can solve different problems.

The distinction is particularly important when success changes the product itself. Kickstarter's experimental first donations helped some projects attract subsequent backing. In an ordinary business, an early customer might also finance an improvement that makes the next customer happier. The advantage can become productive. By the time we inspect the business, asking whether it is good and asking how it became good are separate inquiries.[^i5]

I will take the work seriously throughout. An explanation that erases the engineering, practice, organization, and willingness to bear losses explains too little. So does an explanation that treats the finished reward as a precise measure of those contributions. The task is to understand how real productive activity interacts with the opportunities through which it develops and becomes visible.

### Who gets a chance

A first success can change the opportunities available afterward. The Kickstarter experiment gave researchers a way to observe that process by assigning early advantages. Later donors could respond sensibly to the record they encountered while helping widen the gap between projects.

Each choice does two things: it allocates resources now and helps determine what the next observer will be able to inspect. An expanding record of success can therefore coexist with fewer opportunities to evaluate alternatives.

The comparison budget gives that inquiry a defined object. At each allocation, given the history so far, identify the candidate with the greatest chance of receiving the reward. Take the chance that someone else receives it instead. Accumulate those chances across the opportunities under study. The favorite can change. This measures the allocation chances left outside the current favorite, rather than the number of times an outsider actually wins.[^i2]

A list of awards and rejections does not, by itself, tell us those chances. The budget is not a count of winners or the number of firms on a website. To apply the idea empirically, we need evidence about the allocation process: which alternatives are eligible, what changes their prospects, and what is recorded beyond the award. The quantities cannot be read straight from market shares.

MusicLab helps us see why a catalogue is insufficient. A title remaining on a list does not mean listeners encounter it on comparable terms. Conversely, a single business can arrange useful tests of alternatives, as eBay did with advertising. Counting organizations and counting informative opportunities are different inquiries. Institutional design can matter as much as the number of logos on the screen.

In my allocation models, the comparison budget limits the information the reward record can provide about productive input effects. Under further conditions, it can remain finite over the whole future of a market. Then adding transactions need not let an observer reliably settle attribution. This is a conditional result about a specified experiment, including its information and rules. It is not a declaration that a dominant company is permanently inexplicable.[^i2]

The small word "finite" does substantial work. A tiny but persistent chance of selecting an outsider can keep accumulating. A shrinking chance can also accumulate without limit if it shrinks slowly enough. Even then, comparisons must distinguish the input effects we care about. We will investigate both possibilities before using the result to discuss actual institutions.

Another transaction can demonstrate that an established service continues to work well while revealing little about an untested alternative. Commercial value and information about a particular comparison are separate properties of the same event.

Useful evidence may exist elsewhere: a field experiment, an independent technical evaluation, or measured outcomes beyond the award itself. If those observations distinguish the contribution explanations, they belong in the inquiry. An argument about missing evidence would be worthless if it manufactured scarcity by discarding evidence that exists.

eBay's experiments illustrate this constructive response. The company could change where advertising appeared and observe what followed. Its advertising ledger did not have to remain the last word. Before declaring an attribution problem intractable, we should ask what a feasible intervention could reveal. The comparison budget helps diagnose a specified record; it does not forbid investigators from improving their experiment.

### Other possible histories

The seed of this book's name was a phrase in Steven Johnson's account of Stuart A. Kauffman's adjacent possible. Kauffman, author of *At Home in the Universe*, explored self-organization and complexity. The adjacent possible describes possibilities reachable from what already exists. Johnson called it “a kind of shadow future.” Existing materials and discoveries put some next steps within reach; taking them can make further combinations possible.[^i12]

Think of Bell's transistor. An invention can become a component in devices its inventors never built. Who can work with it matters to which possibilities are explored. Johnson's image made me ask an economic question: what happens to possibilities that depend on opportunities going to someone who never gets them?

This book is itself a product of the adjacent possible. Kauffman's work, Johnson's image, and the economic literature made a further question available to me. Its future will depend on who encounters it and what they can do with it. A professional economist may give these ideas a larger audience, sooner than I can. Credentials, an established readership, and access to the right conversations can open doors that an argument alone cannot. That possibility belongs inside this book, too.

If someone else tests the idea more rigorously, explains it better, or helps more people use it, I will be content with that outcome. Credit matters, as does the freedom to challenge a claim. What I do not want is an epistemic monopoly over an idea that could benefit humanity. I want the comparison to remain open, including when an alternative to my account proves better. I cannot ask readers to take shadow futures seriously and then assume that the history giving me the most recognition must be the best one.

I use shadow futures for feasible alternative allocation histories. They share underlying economic conditions, starting points, and response rules. Different early allocations can produce different later investment, learning, and performance under those same rules. Kauffman's concept concerns expanding possibilities. My narrower use concerns alternative histories and the evidence one realized history supplies about contribution.[^i2]

MusicLab makes those alternatives visible. Lockdown could win or lose without becoming another recording. Ordinary markets rarely keep such convenient parallel charts. An opportunity withheld ten years ago cannot simply be restored with its original circumstances intact.

An imagined better outcome is not evidence. We need a feasible route and a defensible account of how people respond. Changing an early award within unchanged rules explores another history. Changing the allocation rules is an intervention. The eBay experiments changed an arrangement; they did not replay an industry.

A simulation needs the same discipline. Programmed markets reveal consequences of their assumptions; explaining an actual buyer requires evidence that those assumptions fit.

There is another distinction we will need: predicting what comes next and explaining why the outcome has the shape it does. In Lockdown's winning world, an observer who expects more listeners to choose an already popular song could make useful forecasts. Those forecasts would not reveal the other world's chart. Getting the next choice right does not itself account for the song's lead.

Blackwell and Dubins's work helps make the statistical distinction precise: forecasts can converge under specified conditions. Whether the same record identifies productive contribution is another question. We will examine the circumstances in which the two inquiries diverge.[^i2]

Merton described how scientific recognition could bring resources for further work. Alchian examined economic selection without perfect foresight. Research on increasing returns, reinforced processes, and statistical learning helps explain how a history of rewards develops. The comparison-budget bound is my proposed way of connecting those processes to the precision with which contribution can be measured.[^i6]

An early research grant makes the stakes concrete. Thijs Bol, Mathijs de Vaan, and Arnout van de Rijt studied Dutch scientists whose applications fell just above or below a funding threshold. The near-winners subsequently accumulated more than twice as much further funding over eight years as the near-losers, excluding the initial grant itself. Part of the gap arose because unsuccessful applicants entered later funding competitions less often. The distinction involved who continued to present work for evaluation, as well as how evaluators treated it.[^i11]

The research proposal that never reaches another panel leaves a different kind of absence from the one a rejected proposal leaves. We can inspect the latter decision. The former requires asking which earlier events affected participation. When opportunity influences the decision to compete again, a record of later funding awards can conceal part of the process producing its own pool of candidates. That is a reason to follow the people outside the winner's history, even when the published results look complete.

### From evidence to institutions

At the customer-support desk studied by Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond, AI assistance helped workers resolve more issues per hour on average. The gains were particularly strong among less experienced and lower-skilled workers. That is evidence of useful capability reaching people who could benefit from it. It should count in any serious discussion of the technology.[^i7]

But suppose we want to know how organizations should train future workers. Measuring the gain from an assistant available today does not finish the inquiry. We would need to compare arrangements that affect which tasks beginners attempt, what guidance they receive, and what they learn over time. A service that solves the current customer's problem and an institution that develops tomorrow's expertise can be evaluated together, provided we actually preserve evidence about both.

METR's developer research exposes one difficulty. As programmers became less willing to do certain work without AI, the research team found that participation and task selection made its follow-up estimates difficult to interpret. The work being studied was changing which comparisons people would supply. The team responded by reconsidering its design. Learning about the technology required attention to the institutions producing evidence about it.[^i8]

This is one reason the future of economics belongs in a book about AI. More computing can make some experiments cheaper and some records easier to analyze. It can also make one system so convenient that its alternatives are rarely tried. A larger stream of answers may reveal much about current use while leaving important alternatives unresolved. What we learn will depend on questions, access, and design as well as on processing power.

Bell's 1956 settlement provides a historical warning against treating access as a slogan. Opening existing patents to royalty-free licensing was followed by greater innovation outside telecommunications. The study did not find the same result inside the sector, where barriers to reaching customers remained. A policy can loosen one constraint while leaving another in place. An entrant needs a usable route from permission to performance.[^i9]

For antitrust, that suggests an inquiry into what a restriction prevents other people from learning. The relevant harm could extend beyond a higher price today if lost comparisons impair valuable decisions tomorrow. Existing competition policy already considers innovation and other dimensions of competition. My proposal concerns the evidence produced through access, with the practical costs of preserving or replacing it included in the judgment.

For taxation, two questions must remain distinct. One concerns the confidence with which a reward can be divided into contribution and positional advantage. Another concerns whether an action that removes useful comparisons imposes costs on others. The first limits a claim about exact attribution. The second could support a corrective policy only with additional evidence about consequences, incentives, and feasible instruments. Neither question supplies a tax schedule by itself.[^i2]

Policy must compare feasible arrangements. An independent test, better access to existing records, and a change in market rules produce different evidence at different costs. A feasible alternative, a probable improvement, and a worthwhile policy are different judgments. Taking an alternative seriously includes allowing evidence to defeat our hopes for it.

### A different account of success

At Rothamsted, continuing agricultural experiments make the value of maintained comparison unusually tangible. Different treatments have been preserved while methods have also been adapted to keep the experiments relevant. The record matters because someone has continued the arrangements that produce it. Useful knowledge has a material history: plots, treatments, samples, labor, and decisions about which differences to keep.[^i10]

An economy cannot turn every field into an experiment or give every rejected supplier a second chance. Nor should it. The interesting question is where preserving an alternative would teach us enough to improve what comes next. Sometimes the answer will be an independent route into a market. Sometimes it will be an experiment inside a large organization. Sometimes it will be a better measurement, and sometimes the available evidence will support doing nothing.

We will begin with the explanations attached to winners, then examine the comparison budget and shadow futures more closely. The historical chapters ask how opportunities harden into infrastructure and who controls access to a test. The AI chapters follow the investment that makes a model possible, its repeated use, and the workers whose abilities develop alongside it. The final chapters consider what competition policy and taxation can reasonably do with the argument.

Throughout, a winner will remain a winner. Lockdown attracted listeners in the world where it finished first. The AI-assisted customer-support workers resolved problems. What changes is the inference we draw from an outcome: how far it explains itself, which further comparisons would matter, and which institutional choices can be justified with the knowledge available.

The eBay customer gives us a modest version of that discipline. A complete record of the paid click could have encouraged a confident story about the advertisement. Making another route observable changed the explanation. In more consequential settings, we should be equally interested in the route that never became a record. The first task is to notice when the winner's evidence has become the only evidence we allow ourselves to see.
