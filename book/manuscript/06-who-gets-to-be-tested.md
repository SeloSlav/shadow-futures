# Chapter 5

## Who gets to be tested

### Before the first query

The choice can be made before you type.

Open a browser on a new device and enter a question into its address bar. Unless you have changed the settings, a search engine has already been selected to receive it. You can have a strong preference for that engine, no preference, or no clear idea how the selection happened. In each case, the question goes somewhere, a result comes back, and a little more experience accumulates around the service that received it.

In the Google search case, that apparently ordinary sequence became the subject of an extraordinary record. Browser developers, device manufacturers, and wireless carriers had agreements governing default search placement. Google's payments for distribution exceeded $26 billion in 2021, according to the district court's findings. Default access was valuable enough to command an enormous price even when a user paid nothing to enter a query.[^c5-1]

The court's August 2024 opinion credited Google's skilled engineers, continuing innovation, and high search quality. It also found that Google had unlawfully maintained monopolies in general search services and general search text advertising through exclusive distribution agreements.[^c5-1] Building a useful service and controlling a rival's route to customers are different activities. We can reward the first while restricting the second.

Distribution also shapes what a search engine gets to learn. A query is an opportunity to serve someone. It can also generate information about what people want and how well the service responds. A distribution arrangement can therefore influence both today's allocation of attention and the evidence available for tomorrow's improvement. The advantage does not have to remain a matter of habit. It can help make the favored service better.

That possibility should make the question more interesting, not easier. If an engine learns through use, a rival may need use to develop. Yet a user can reasonably prefer the engine that has already developed. The established provider's present quality and the rival's difficulty acquiring experience can reinforce each other. We cannot resolve that sequence by announcing that quality matters, or that exposure matters, and refusing to consider the other.

The phone also brings us back to Bell. A route to the customer can govern which alternatives produce a record. For the telephone attachment, the route involved permission to use a device. For a search engine, it can involve the place where a question first arrives. The technologies and legal cases differ. The economic question travels: who gets a meaningful opportunity to be used, improved, and evaluated?

An answer requires more than counting search engines. We need to follow the question through the system. Which service receives it? Which service generates the results? Who observes the response? Who can use that response to improve? Different arrangements can give different answers, even when the screen offers the same number of brand names.

### Firefox tries another default

The public record contains a useful change of route. From 2014 through 2017, Firefox's default search engine was Yahoo. Previously it had been Google. According to the court's account, Google's share of Firefox queries fell abruptly from roughly eighty to ninety percent to roughly sixty to seventy percent. Yahoo's share rose from around ten percent to around thirty. In 2017, when the default returned to Google, the query shares moved back.[^c5-2]

The users were not compelled to stay with either provider. Some evidently took a route different from the default. That did not make the default irrelevant. Its importance lay in how the arrangement changed the distribution of queries among people who retained the ability to choose. A friction does not have to be an absolute prohibition to affect who receives an opportunity.

This was an observed change in an actual browser, rather than a clean randomized experiment. Search quality, advertising, and commercial terms also belonged to the history. The court described Yahoo increasing advertising to meet its payment commitment, worsening the experience and contributing to Mozilla's decision to change back. The switch cannot be turned into a timeless estimate of what every default does in every setting.[^c5-2]

What it does give us is a concrete contrast. A statement that Google received most Firefox queries has a different meaning once we know that changing the preselected engine changed the shares. The outcome reflects an arrangement as well as individual responses within it. A responsible explanation should investigate both.

There is another lesson in the reversal. An opportunity to reach users can expose weaknesses in the service that receives it. Yahoo's place in the story does not establish that giving a rival distribution necessarily produces a superior product. The additional use can reveal a problem, finance an improvement, or change the commercial pressure on the provider. Preserving an informative opportunity does not dictate which of those results should emerge.

A successful experiment can strengthen the case for an incumbent. Conversely, a change of supplier can follow a poorly designed trial. The identity of the eventual winner cannot establish whether useful learning occurred. That requires examining the test, its outcomes, and the decision it informed.

Firefox's actual history also helps distinguish a changed rule from a replay. Changing the default changed the arrangement through which queries were distributed. It was an intervention. A shadow future under the old arrangement would hold the relevant rules steady and follow another feasible sequence. Both comparisons can help us think, but the evidence from a changed default answers a question about that change.

### When a query teaches a search engine

The district court described several ways that user activity helped search. Queries reveal what people ask. Clicks, returns to the results page, and other interactions can help assess relevance. Query data can help decide what belongs in an index and which pages need to be revisited. More use can improve the work of finding and presenting information.[^c5-3]

An unusual query makes that advantage tangible. An engine cannot learn from a question it never receives. Nor does one encounter necessarily reveal which result was useful. A click, an immediate return, and a later reformulation can tell different stories. The court described rankings informed by accumulated interactions. Scale supplies more than a larger inbox: it can supply repeated occasions to learn which answers people find useful.[^c5-3]

This is productive information. It would be a mistake to describe every additional query as empty repetition. The relevant distinction is what a query helps us learn. More encounters can improve the established engine's response to encountered tasks while leaving another question less well answered: what would a different service, or a different development route, achieve with a meaningful chance to operate?

A search result also reflects several decisions. The provider has chosen which pages to collect, how to rank them, and how to present the answer. A user's response concerns what was shown under those choices. It does not directly report the response to every result that might have been shown by another system. The record can be rich and still have an uneven relationship to the comparisons we want.

That unevenness gives the word “data” too much work to do. A catalogue of pages, a sequence of user interactions, and results produced by another company's engine are different resources. Each can help a competitor in a different way. Handing over one does not automatically hand over the capability to generate the others. The remedy proceedings eventually had to address these distinctions in practical detail.

Our comparison budget asks a narrower allocation question: how much modeled chance accumulates outside the current favorite? It does not count queries, bytes, or people. Applying the model would require a defined allocation unit, a contribution target, justified probabilities, and an account of all informative observations. The court's search record is not an estimate of that budget, and a large search share does not establish that its lifetime total is finite.

The favorite here is the alternative most likely to receive the next allocation under the model. It can change with history. A service's share of past searches is an outcome, not that conditional probability; turning one into the other would require separately justified evidence and assumptions.

The historical mechanism and the model can nevertheless direct attention to a common practical problem. Opportunities to serve users affect what providers can learn and become. A challenger can be told to improve before receiving use, while useful improvement partly depends on receiving use. An institution concerned with competition has to examine how that loop works, alongside the benefits that existing scale supplies.

### The comparison inside Google

The court record includes a particularly useful complication for a book about missing comparisons. Google evaluated its search quality against other engines using human-rated, side-by-side studies. It also compared its speed with Bing's. A 2017 comparison found a latency disadvantage and helped prompt Project Folly, an effort to reduce delays. The court described the project as successful.[^c5-5]

This is evidence of comparison inside the dominant organization. The provider could identify a weakness, examine a rival's performance, and improve. We should not discard that activity because the organization was large, profitable, or later found liable for unlawful conduct. Those are different facts about the same institution. An account of what it knows has to include the tests it actually conducts.

The example sharpens the question for outsiders. A test of search speed can answer a useful question about search speed. It need not reveal how another provider would develop after several years of different distribution. Nor does it automatically establish the contribution of every input to Google's accumulated rewards. The comparison should receive credit for what it investigates, while leaving other questions open.

For a user waiting for results, a faster response is a real improvement. For a potential entrant, a public demonstration of a faster prototype may still fall short of obtaining a route to users. Both perspectives matter. A system can support productive internal learning while making some kinds of external development difficult. That combination is more informative than a choice between declaring the incumbent all-knowing and declaring that it learns nothing.

Project Folly offers a more useful test than a count of experiments. Google's comparison identified a concrete disadvantage, and work followed to reduce it. An outsider appraising that evidence should ask whether latency is the uncertainty affecting the decision. If so, the test matters. If the question concerns another provider's development under different distribution, measuring response time cannot answer it alone.

We should also distinguish a test's existence from access to its results. A private organization can possess useful evidence that a buyer, researcher, or public authority cannot examine. That can create problems of verification, incentives, or dependence. It does not justify pretending the evidence was never generated. The next step is to specify who needs to know what, and why.

### A name for control over knowledge

Zahra Meghani called this kind of control an “epistemic monopoly.” Her 2021 paper examined regulatory agencies' dependence on product developers for the knowledge needed to evaluate technological products. She argued for access to credible sources beyond the sponsor and examined how broad patent rights could let a developer control conditions of independent research.[^c5-6]

For a particular question about contribution, epistemic monopoly means control over the opportunities needed to compare alternatives. An actor that controls who gets tested and who can inspect the results can help determine which explanations of the rewards will ever receive a hearing.

The distinction helps us ask something precise about the search case. Distribution contracts can affect who receives queries. Data terms can affect who learns from an existing record. A test's confidentiality can affect who can check the evidence. These controls can overlap, but they are not the same control. Each needs to be connected to the particular uncertainty under investigation.

Verifying invoices, hours, and completed work establishes inputs and execution. Identifying their effects requires suitable comparisons. Access to an existing informative experiment is a different problem from arranging an experiment that has never occurred. Those obstacles call for different responses.

This is why an information argument should begin with a question rather than a label. Which contribution are we trying to understand? Which observations could distinguish the relevant explanations? Who controls the route through which those observations would arise? The term becomes useful when it organizes that investigation. It becomes an accusation without content when it merely replaces “large technology company” with a more alarming phrase.

### The product on the bench

Meghani's discussion has a physical counterpart to the search record: a product that outside scientists need to study. Her paper examined a dispute over the regulation of dicamba herbicides. The underlying court opinion, issued in June 2020, described Monsanto's field studies in support of an initial 2016 registration. The company had tested its reformulated herbicide in Georgia and Texas. At that time, it had not made the herbicide available for independent studies.[^c5-16]

There were results, then, and there were experiments. The problem was not an empty file. A developer had produced evidence about its own product, while another route to producing evidence was unavailable. That is different from saying the developer's measurements must have been false. It asks what could be learned by investigators able to choose and carry out their own tests.

The court later vacated the challenged 2018 registrations on grounds concerning the agency's treatment of risks. It did not base that decision simply on a rule that every product lacked satisfactory data unless independently tested. The historical detail relevant here is the organization of inquiry before the earlier registration, not a general judgment about every herbicide or a statement of current regulatory status.[^c5-16]

A scientist with permission to read a company's white paper and a scientist with permission to test the product have different opportunities. The first can examine what the reported study says. The second can investigate another condition, reproduce a result, or find that the first study left a consequential question unanswered. The work may confirm the developer's conclusion. Independence matters because it permits a relevant inquiry, including one the developer did not select.

The example also shows why disclosure and access should be connected. Without enough information about methods, an outside scientist may struggle to reproduce a study. Without the product, a detailed method may remain something that cannot be attempted. An effective arrangement has to follow the practical requirements of the investigation. The existence of published pages does not establish that every important question can be checked.

Disclosure is useful when the existing record answers the question. Releasing another copy of a performance report cannot replace a failure condition that was never tested. The first task is to identify whether the missing element is access, interpretation, or another observation.

Expertise, instruments, and experience matter. An independent test can be incompetent, redundant, or badly designed. The institutional objective is to enable credible investigation of consequential questions and to assess its results, rather than to treat independence as a substitute for scientific quality.

### More signs, one route

The historical concern is the reach of a judgment. A rejection by one independent buyer can leave other buyers free to form their own assessments. A shared ranking can transmit that rejection across a much larger market. Demand can expand geographically while the routes to independent evaluation contract.

Shared services can also reduce prices, improve reliability, and connect entrants to distant customers. The question is whether those gains preserve a practical route to another judgment. Multiple websites and separate owners do not establish that independence by themselves.

Search syndication gives this distinction a real institutional form. A service can obtain search results from another provider and present them through its own product. This can make entry feasible without duplicating an entire search infrastructure. It also means that several user-facing services need not represent several independently developed engines. Their relationship has to be described before their number is treated as evidence of independent discovery.[^c5-7]

The reverse possibility matters too. A single operator might maintain genuinely informative trials of different approaches. Project Folly is a reminder that a large provider can learn through comparison, although its particular tests do not answer every question. Counting organizations could miss that activity just as counting storefronts could exaggerate the independence of their underlying systems.

None of this makes organizational structure irrelevant. Separate firms can bring different incentives, technical judgments, and routes to users. Common ownership can make some questions less attractive to investigate. The point is to establish which differences produce the evidence we need, rather than assuming that every legal boundary creates another informative history or that every internal boundary destroys one.

For a regulator or buyer, the useful map therefore follows functions. Who develops the service? Who distributes it? Who records outcomes? Who can change the experiment? Who can examine the result? The same company name can appear in several boxes. Different names can lead back to the same underlying process. The map tells us more than a count of badges on a screen.

### Three different doors

The September 2025 remedies opinion and the December final judgment addressed several routes at once. The court did not require Google to sell Chrome. It restricted specified distribution conditions, required particular forms of data access, and provided for search syndication. These were distinct interventions with different objects. A headline announcing that Google must share would not tell a rival what it could actually obtain.[^c5-7]

One door concerned distribution. The judgment restricted tying the licensing of Google applications to the placement of specified Google services and restricted arrangements preventing partners from offering rivals. It also imposed one-year limits on certain agreements. These provisions concerned the route through which a user encounters a service. They did not themselves give the rival a finished search engine.

A second door concerned data. The judgment specified information from Google's web index and particular user-interaction datasets used in ranking models. An index is a structured record of what the service has found on the web. User interactions can inform relevance and training. Giving a rival access to these resources could help it develop a service, but the judgment did not simply hand over all Google's algorithms, ranking signals, or trained models.[^c5-7]

The web-index provisions named concrete fields: document identifiers, their corresponding web addresses, when an address was first seen and last crawled, a spam score, and the device type. They also included information identifying documents Google considered duplicates. These are useful ingredients for another provider's work. They are not an instruction to make the same ranking decision. Nor did the access order transfer the intellectual property rights of third parties whose material appeared in the index. An entrant could still need machinery for collecting pages, interpreting them, and delivering results. The list makes the distinction between knowledge access and a finished capability inspectable: we can ask which specified resource removes which obstacle, rather than treat the whole search engine as a single indivisible secret.[^c5-7]

The third door concerned an operating service: syndication. A qualified competitor could obtain specified search results and features through an interface, subject to the judgment's terms. This allows a provider to use capabilities it has not reproduced for itself. It can lower the burden of serving users while the provider develops. It also raises a question about how the arrangement supports eventual independent capability.

The judgment limited reliance on that route. First-year Google syndication was capped at forty percent of a qualified competitor's annual US queries, with tapering over five years intended to encourage investment in independent search capabilities. The court left the pace and measurement of tapering for further determination. Access supplied a bridge; the terms sought to keep the bridge from becoming the competitor's permanent foundation. Whether that would work required evidence from implementation.[^c5-7]

Market access, access to knowledge, and permission to use a technical service change different opportunities. A measure that opens one route can leave the others closed. Evaluation needs to follow the resulting ability to develop and test alternatives.

This is where a comparison perspective becomes more demanding than a general endorsement of openness. Which door produces the evidence relevant to the decision? If the buyer wants to compare interfaces, access to the same underlying service might suffice. If it wants to compare independent methods of generating results, that arrangement may preserve the very dependence being investigated. A remedy must be connected to the question it is expected to help answer.

The court's findings of legal violations supplied the basis for intervention. Choosing a remedy raises a further practical question: will a rival gain a workable way to produce evidence that could change a buyer's choice?

### The cost of opening them

Search payments also financed something outside search. The court's 2024 opinion reported that Google's payment to Mozilla exceeded $400 million in 2021 and represented about eighty percent of Mozilla's operating budget. The financial connection supporting Google's default position also helped support a separate browser. A change directed at one market could therefore affect the resources available in another.[^c5-8]

Mozilla's chief executive, Laura Chambers, welcomed the court's rejection of a proposed ban on search payments in September 2025. She argued that a ban would weaken independent browsers. She also welcomed greater flexibility to offer other search and AI services and emphasized the need for careful implementation of data remedies. These were the arguments of an interested participant, with its own experience and incentives, rather than an independent welfare verdict.[^c5-9]

The position is economically intelligible. A browser is the software through which a person accesses the web; a search engine supplies answers to searches. More competition in one does not automatically finance competition in the other. A payment can help sustain a browser while also making a particular search relationship financially attractive. The arrangement has to be examined across both activities.

The court considered the risk that a broad payment ban would harm distributors or leave them using Google without receiving revenue. It declined to impose the requested blanket ban. That conclusion did not mean every distribution restriction was acceptable. It meant the remedy had to be assessed in the system where its effects would unfold.[^c5-10]

For a comparison policy, the lesson is that one route can support another. Removing a financial tie might open a search opportunity while weakening a browser through which alternatives reach users. Keeping the tie might preserve that browser while maintaining incentives unfavorable to rival search providers. Describing either move as an unqualified increase in competition would skip the interaction that makes the choice difficult.

A remedy can also remove useful experimentation financed by an incumbent's existing business. Any appraisal must count evidence lost as well as gained, alongside changes in production, financing, and access.

This is not a demand that every alternative pay for itself immediately. A comparison can have benefits beyond its first transaction. It is a demand to identify those benefits and their costs. Hayek's discovery argument gives us a reason to value processes whose particular results are unknown in advance. It does not give us a reason to assume that every rearrangement of those processes increases the value of what gets discovered.[^c5-11]

### Who can examine the test

Data-sharing disputes make the same problem concrete in a different way. A search record can contain sensitive information even when a name has been removed. In the remedies opinion, the court treated privacy as a valid concern and discussed techniques for reducing disclosure risks. The final judgment required safeguards, licensing terms, and information about the contents and preparation of the datasets.[^c5-12]

These requirements belong inside the informational analysis. Data made safer to share can become less useful for some purposes. A record whose preparation nobody understands can be difficult to interpret. A technically useful record might still be inappropriate for unrestricted circulation. The objective is a defensible opportunity to learn, with the people represented in the record included in the accounting.

The judgment's definition of a qualified competitor also involved more than asking for a copy. It included approved data-security standards, privacy and security audits, and a sufficient plan to invest and compete in or with the specified markets. Access was an administered relationship with obligations. It was not a public download button available to anyone who described themselves as a challenger.[^c5-7]

Such conditions can make a remedy safer and more purposeful. They can also affect who can use it. A small organization may lack the capacity to meet requirements that a large rival can satisfy. That possibility does not prove the requirements are wrong. It tells the designer to examine whether they are necessary and proportionate, and whether a less costly arrangement could preserve their function.

Mozilla's response raised a related concern: data remedies should avoid simply transferring power from one major technology company to another. That was a stakeholder's warning about design. It deserves consideration alongside the potential benefit of making useful resources available. The name of the recipient cannot substitute for examining what the recipient will be able to test and how others will benefit.[^c5-9]

For a public evaluator, access to a test and access to its results are likewise separate. It may be possible to arrange an independent evaluation without releasing every underlying record publicly. It may be necessary to disclose methods so that a result can be interpreted. A confidentiality condition might protect legitimate information or prevent meaningful scrutiny. The right response follows the specific obstacle and purpose.

A published pass score may conceal performance on rare but costly failures. Detailed results could resolve that uncertainty without exposing every line of code. If the relevant condition was never tested, however, fuller disclosure cannot produce the missing result.

That distinction separates two forms of control that often travel together. One governs what happens and therefore what evidence is generated. The other governs who can inspect the evidence afterward. An institution can open its files while restricting new inquiry, or permit inquiry while keeping useful results difficult to check. A serious account of epistemic power must follow both.

### A screen that can be tested

Mozilla's 2023 study randomly varied browser choice screens during simulated device setup in Germany, Spain, and Poland. Information, list length, and timing were experimental choices. One comparison held the five-browser screen constant while changing when it appeared.[^c5-13]

When the screen followed a click on the preset browser's icon, roughly nineteen percent selected that browser, compared with eleven percent during setup. The preset was Samsung Internet or Edge. The preceding click was a proposed explanation, not a separately established mechanism.[^c5-13]

Chrome was an alternative here. Timing could favor a position occupied by another brand.

These simulated choices do not establish sustained browser use or independent search capability. A remedy's sequence can nevertheless affect its immediate outcome.

The study changed a rule and observed responses. It therefore supplies evidence about an intervention. A shadow future would preserve the old rules while outcomes unfolded differently. The distinction keeps us from asking one experiment to settle both how a screen works and how a whole competitive history would have developed.

There is a constructive implication for the institution imposing a remedy. It can ask how to learn whether the remedy works. Which outcomes would show that a meaningful opportunity had opened? Which would indicate that users were merely clicking through another screen? What evidence would distinguish a temporary shift from a sustained improvement? Those questions should influence the record collected as the remedy operates.

They also create a route for revising an intervention that disappoints. A choice screen might need different information, a different timing, or a different role within a wider package. A trial can increase confidence in an incumbent or identify a problem with a challenger. An institution that values evidence should be able to learn both kinds of lesson without treating them as failures of principle.

### The work after the judgment

The subsequent Google record brings implementation back into view. In their September 17, 2026 status report, the plaintiffs described the work of the technical committee, the development of processes, and efforts to implement the final judgment. The report discussed template licenses, requests for information, and exchanges over proposed terms. An obligation to provide access had become a sequence of organizational and technical tasks.[^c5-14]

This was the plaintiffs' account, rather than a court finding that every obligation had been fulfilled. It describes the practical route to access: who qualifies, which records can be used, how privacy is protected, what documentation is supplied, and which terms remain disputed. A rival's opportunity depends on how those details are settled.

A deadline in a status report is a plan, not a completed exchange. The proposed dates for templates and responses did not show that every later step had occurred or that a rival had received useful data. To judge the remedy, we would need to follow those steps through to their effects.

The legal dispute continued beyond the district court's decision. Google publicly announced an appeal in January 2026 and argued that the ruling discounted user preference, product quality, and competition. It also objected to the data and syndication requirements. In July, the plaintiffs' appellate brief defended relief and challenged the rejection of a payment ban. These were opposing parties' arguments, rather than an appellate resolution.[^c5-15]

That continuing disagreement matters for interpretation. We should not describe a contested remedy as a completed experiment whose results are already known. We can examine the theory of what it is intended to change, the terms actually ordered, and the evidence produced as it operates. Later results could support, complicate, or undermine expectations on either side.

A formal promise of access becomes economically meaningful through specifications, a budget, security arrangements, usable records, and a way to interpret outcomes. Those implementation requirements help determine whether comparison actually occurs.

The same attention should be given to a claim that a remedy has failed. A route could be open but unattractive because the alternative is poor. It could be attractive but burdened by unnecessary conditions. It could be operating while its learning benefits take time to appear. The investigator needs evidence capable of distinguishing these accounts, rather than choosing one from the identity of the winner.

### Before a rival can grow

An alternative can disappear before it leaves enough evidence to judge. A database of completed transactions will contain no entry for a product whose development stopped before its first sale. Sharing the incumbent's records cannot recreate the engineers' unencountered problems, the customers' unmade requests, or the improvements that might have followed. Existing knowledge and opportunities to produce new knowledge require different remedies.

Matthew's Gospel tells a brutal story of preemption. Herod hears of a child described as a future king and seeks to eliminate the threat before it can grow. In Matthew's account, he orders the killing of Bethlehem's young boys, while the child he seeks has been taken to Egypt. The threatened successor is to be removed before he can become a rival. The economic question is what happens when an incumbent can close a challenger's route before there is a performance to compare.[^c5-herod]

That question already has an economic literature. Colleen Cunningham, Florian Ederer, and Song Ma studied pharmaceutical acquisitions and subsequent project development. Acquired projects overlapping the buyer's existing drugs were less likely to continue development; the pattern was stronger where competitive pressure was weak. Their research investigates a mechanism through which buying a potential competitor can prevent competition from arriving. These are empirical findings with identification assumptions, not a randomized test of every acquisition.[^c5-preemption]

An acquisition can also finance development that would otherwise stop. Counting today's competing products can miss the consequences of either decision: continued funding may let a project mature, while closure may remove a chance to learn what the entrant could have built.

Peter Thiel makes a forceful case for monopoly in *Zero to One*, written with Blake Masters. In an essay adapted from the book, he compares competitive equilibrium to the "heat death of the universe." Durable monopoly profits, he argues, support ambitious research and long-term plans. His account anticipates new innovators replacing established monopolists.[^c5-thiel]

The difficulty begins with the route to becoming that replacement. A company can finance useful research while controlling the customers, interfaces, or tests a different research program needs. Its next invention will be visible. The rival's unrealized invention will not appear beside it in the accounts. How would we know whether protecting one laboratory accelerated discovery overall if the same protection prevented another laboratory from getting started?

Google's Project Folly makes the visible side concrete. A comparison exposed a speed disadvantage, and engineering work reduced it.[^c5-5] That improvement matters to users. It does not answer what a different supplier might have learned from a workable route to customers. The two developments could have occurred together, or access could have imposed costs large enough to change the research undertaken. Deciding between those arrangements requires a comparison that counts both development routes.

The mistake is to compare a funded innovator with a world of firms doing identical work on vanishing margins, then treat those as the available choices. An economy can preserve substantial rewards for an invention while requiring interconnection or bounded access that lets someone develop a different one. Commercial breathing room and authority over who may become a competitor are separable parts of the arrangement.

We can follow the dominant firm's research decades into the future and still leave a rival's possible development out of the calculation. The incumbent's acceleration then looks like the whole gain. A slower beginning for one large project might preserve several useful attempts; duplicated effort might also waste resources. The relevant comparison includes those costs and the independent learning they purchase.

A succession of innovators needs a route through which a successor can emerge. When the current winner controls that route, its freedom from competition can erode the process invoked to justify it. The decisive question is whether the next generation can acquire evidence strong enough to challenge the present one.

The pharmaceutical study concerns acquired drug projects; the Google judgment concerns search distribution agreements. Assessing either practice requires evidence about the development route affected, the benefits supplied, and the costs of a feasible alternative. A finding about one practice cannot establish another company's motives.

Requiring a mature product before granting the access needed to develop it protects the very condition preventing the rival from qualifying. Exclusive terms that close such an indispensable route should face a presumption against enforcement, subject to evidence that a narrower arrangement cannot protect legitimate interests. Existing antitrust already considers innovation and exclusion; an information-based remedy still needs an identified lost comparison, a useful decision it could inform, and benefits worth the remedy's costs. Legal liability depends on the applicable law and evidence.

### Whose future is missing?

Return to the address bar. The question you type reaches an engine, and the engine returns a result. That useful act is real. So is the work that made it possible. The institutional question concerns the other routes by which useful acts might be attempted and evaluated, and the arrangements that govern who gets to learn from them.

For a buyer or public authority, the task is to make that question practical. Identify a decision worth improving. Find the comparison that could improve it. Ask who can produce and check the evidence, what doing so costs, and what existing knowledge can already resolve. Sometimes another route to users matters. Sometimes better access to a record or a well-designed internal trial supplies what is needed.

Rewards also carry personal consequences. What does a documented achievement establish about what its makers deserve? Before turning to AI's factories and workplaces, we need to separate evidence about contribution from the moral and practical reasons for rewarding it.
