# Chapter 1

## The winner and the explanation

### An ending looking for a beginning

Put Lockdown at the top of the chart and imagine that the other chart has disappeared. The first thing we know is that listeners selected it. The next thing we may want to know is what made them do so. We could listen for a memorable passage, compare the song with its competitors, and form a view about the audience. A good explanation might begin with something that really is in the recording. The trouble starts when we decide that the ranking has told us how much that feature mattered.

Lockdown's fortieth-place finish exposes the gap between a recording's appeal and its rank. Appeal could help the song in both worlds. The different rankings also depended on the sequence of attention, choices, and displayed popularity it met in each one.[^c1-1]

Follow a MusicLab listener. The website offered unfamiliar songs by unfamiliar bands. A participant chose a recording, listened, and was asked to rate it on a scale from one star to five. Afterward came an opportunity to download it. Downloading was optional. The experiment recorded several steps between a title appearing on a screen and a song acquiring another mark of popularity.[^c1-1]

A rating and a download answered different questions. The rating expressed a response after hearing the recording; in the social-influence worlds, the download entered the popularity record shown to subsequent listeners. Imagine someone who liked a track but chose not to keep it. That person's favorable judgment would not become another download on the chart. Even this simple market contained more than one way of being successful. The researchers had to choose which outcome to explain.

Now change the screen. In the first experiment, songs appeared in a grid, with positions assigned randomly for each participant. Some listeners also saw download counts. In the second, socially influenced listeners saw a single column ordered by popularity. An early download could now help move a title up the list encountered by the next listener. The recording had stayed fixed while its route to an audience changed.[^c1-1]

Think of a title near the bottom. To attract a listener, it has to be noticed among alternatives carrying more evidence of earlier attention. Once heard, it still has to win a download. A final download total combines those stages. A musician interested in reaching listeners would want to know where the obstacle lay: getting the title seen, getting the song played, or getting someone to keep it. The total alone cannot separate them.

Looking first at popular titles can make sense. A listener with ten spare minutes has a practical search problem. Earlier downloads may offer a useful shortcut. But that shortcut helps decide which recordings receive the next hearing. The chart both reports choices and influences the conditions under which further choices occur. It cannot be read as forty-eight recordings repeatedly taking the same unchanged test.

For Lockdown, separate worlds let the researchers investigate what an ordinary chart conceals. Each world began without a download advantage, then acquired its own record. An admirer hearing the first-place song has evidence about an encounter with that recording. The fortieth-place world adds evidence about the process through which encounters accumulate. Enjoying the song answers a real question. Explaining its finishing position requires the wider record that the experiment preserved.

Ordinary economic success comes with a similar invitation. We see the company that reached customers, the worker who developed a reputation, or the project that secured its backing. We can inspect choices those winners made. What is harder to inspect is how another beginning might have changed the circumstances under which the same choices were available or effective. That is why evidence about the beginning deserves attention before we start reading the ending as a verdict.

### Giving success away

The Kickstarter researchers did something direct about beginnings. They intervened before selected projects had raised any money. The projects already had proposals, ambitions, and creators. The researchers did not change those proposals. They changed whether an early visitor encountered a project with a first donation or a project still waiting for one. This allowed later support to be compared with a difference in initial success deliberately introduced by the research design.[^c1-2]

Improving a pitch and adding its first backer could both increase subsequent funding. Without the experimental assignment, an observer might mistake the second mechanism for the first. A creator could offer a polished explanation of why a successful pitch worked, and much of that explanation might be sensible. It would still need to account for the advantage that came from already having support.

The experiment found that projects given an initial donation were more likely to attract subsequent backing. Later support was counted separately from the researchers' donation. It was not enough to announce that the treated projects finished with more money, since the researchers had put money into them. The revealing result concerned what other people did afterward. The design separated the initial gift from the additional support that followed it.

That gives us a useful discipline for other claims about success. When someone says an investment brought a return, ask which part of the return has been counted. A first customer supplies revenue directly and may help attract the next customer. Those are different benefits. If we count the initial payment as evidence of the power to attract later payments, we have smuggled the intervention into the outcome. The Kickstarter design avoided that elementary trap.

The team did not stop at crowdfunding. Its experiments also intervened in product-review ratings, Wikipedia awards, and petition signatures. For Wikipedia, the subjects were highly productive editors; a randomly selected subset received a community award. The researchers then observed further awards from other people. Recognition could influence subsequent recognition even among people who were already contributing substantial work.[^c1-2]

The editor is a particularly instructive figure. The work exists before the experimental award. A later observer who sees several honors can reasonably think the editor has done useful things. That may be entirely correct. What the number of honors cannot establish unaided is how much each later recognition reflects the work and how much reflects the fact that the editor has already been recognized. Genuine contribution and a feedback effect can occupy the same page.

There is also no need to suppose that every later donor or award-giver merely copies a number. Early support can invite closer inspection. Closer inspection can reveal something worth supporting. An institution may benefit from such shortcuts when nobody can investigate every candidate. The experimental result concerns the consequence of receiving the early advantage. Determining the precise channel, and whether it improves decisions, requires further evidence rather than an assumption that influence is automatically foolish.

The researchers' larger interventions supply a useful warning against a seductive sequel. They compared smaller and larger manufactured advantages and found diminishing marginal returns. In the funding treatment, moving from one experimental donor to four did not create a proportionate multiplication of later success. A system can amplify the first step and respond much less strongly to the next few. The force of feedback is a question about its rules and setting.[^c1-2]

That result prevents us from taking the favorite story about a snowball and making it the entire theory. Some early differences persist. Some run out of force. Some are overcome by other differences. If every unequal result were offered as evidence of irresistible cumulative advantage, nothing could count against the idea. The experiments are useful precisely because they can show both amplification and its limits.

For a project creator, the practical difference might be between improving the proposal and finding a way to secure credible initial backing. Both could deserve effort. An observer trying to learn which bottleneck matters should not treat the final funding total as if it had already performed that diagnosis. The first donor has changed the situation in which the next donor decides. Understanding the record means following that change through time.

### The extra vote

A similar problem can begin with something smaller than a donation. In an experiment published in 2013, Lev Muchnik, Sinan Aral, and Sean Taylor randomly gave online comments an initial positive vote, a negative vote, or no vote. Over five months the study covered 101,281 comments on a social news website. The manipulation changed a visible score attached to words that had already been written.[^c1-3]

An artificial positive vote increased the likelihood that the next viewer would vote positively by 32 percent relative to the control group. Final mean scores were 25 percent higher. The percentages describe relative differences in those outcomes; they do not mean that a third of readers had been converted from disagreement to agreement. One extra mark on the screen had changed the subsequent path of evaluation.[^c1-3]

Here the productive object is unusually easy to hold in view. The comment did not become better written when the researchers clicked the positive button. A later reader could still find a good argument in it. But the experimental advantage was not an improvement in the argument. If a final score were used as a precise measure of the quality of the words, some of the effect of the initial vote would be assigned to the writing.

The study's site did not order comments by their popularity. That is a useful detail because it stops us from explaining the whole result as the familiar consequence of being moved higher on a ranked page. The displayed score was tied to the comment when people evaluated it. Social information could affect the subsequent rating even without that particular route through a popularity ranking.[^c1-3]

A comment score can help a reader find something useful while also reflecting the votes earlier readers happened to see. Its convenience and its reliability as a measure of quality depend on different features of the rating process.

Negative votes behaved differently. Readers tended to correct the artificial negative treatment, leaving final mean ratings without a statistically detectable difference from controls. The upward and downward nudges were not symmetrical.[^c1-3]

The topic mattered too. The researchers detected positive herding in politics, culture and society, and business. They did not detect it in economics, information technology, fun, or general news. Those categories had comparable activity levels. The same button and the same visible score did not produce a uniform response across the site.[^c1-3]

Compare the comment with Lockdown. In both settings, someone encounters an object together with information about earlier responses. The object may be appealing. The earlier response may also matter. The observer who sees only the accumulated result faces a mixture of those influences. The two experiments make the mixture easier to study by introducing or separating the relevant histories.

What they establish is specific enough to be useful. An early signal can affect subsequent judgment. It need not determine every outcome, work equally in both directions, or leave the underlying work unchanged in a longer economic setting. We have evidence for a mechanism and reasons to investigate its strength. We do not yet have a general permission to read all success as accidental or all rankings as worthless.

### Recognition becomes an opportunity

In his account of scientific recognition, Robert Merton used the image of a forty-first chair. The French Academy had forty places, but the history of French thought contained distinguished people who never occupied one. Merton's examples included Descartes, Molière, and Proust. An institution with a fixed number of honors cannot turn the boundary between its members and everyone else into a complete ranking of achievement.[^c1-4]

There are forty available chairs. An outstanding person can remain outside without anyone needing to conclude that the work was poor. A prize can be prestigious because its recipients are excellent, while failing to include everyone of comparable excellence. The quality of the winners and the completeness of the distinction are separate properties.

Merton went further. Recognition could become a resource for future research. Eminent scientists could receive disproportionate credit for contributions, and credit could help bring facilities for further work. His argument drew in part on Harriet Zuckerman's interviews with Nobel laureates. The honor was both an account of earlier achievement and a possible influence on what the scientist could do next.[^c1-4]

That second role changes the economic question. An extra chair would alter an honor roll. Access to a laboratory can alter the work that later appears on the roll. If we inspect achievement only after the opportunities have diverged, we may find a real difference in productive capacity. The difference can be an outcome of the earlier allocation as well as a reason for the later one.

The Dutch grant study introduced earlier makes this process less dependent on a striking phrase. Bol, de Vaan, and van de Rijt examined applicants close to a funding threshold. Those just above it later accumulated more than twice as much additional grant money over eight years as those just below. The original award was excluded from that total. A narrow early distinction was followed by a much wider difference in further funding.[^c1-5]

The design matters as much as the result. Comparing all successful applicants with all rejected applicants would entangle the award with differences that helped determine who won. Focusing on applicants near the threshold makes the comparison more credible under the study's assumptions. It does not establish that every pair of nearby scientists possessed identical talent. It provides a way to investigate an early award's consequences without treating the entire difference in later records as proof of initial superiority.

The competitions had names: Veni for the early-career award and Vidi for the midcareer grant. Among applicants closest to the Veni threshold, about 59 percent of winners later applied for Vidi, compared with 40 percent of nonwinners. The researchers checked whether the application difference simply reflected people leaving academia or the country; it did not. An earlier decision had changed which proposals reached a later panel, before that panel judged anything.[^c1-5]

A scientist can stop applying, a supplier can stop bidding, and a musician can stop trying to reach an audience. The alternatives do not wait patiently for evaluation. An apparently thin field may reflect earlier decisions that drove people away. The missing proposal is harder to inspect than one that received a low score.

There are several possible practical responses, and they need different evidence. Helping promising unsuccessful applicants reapply would address participation. Giving a reviewer a clearer account of how close a prior decision was might affect the interpretation of a failure. Funding everyone would involve a much larger commitment with different costs. The study makes a mechanism visible; choosing a response requires asking which change would improve the institution's work.

Subsequent funding gives a scientist resources for further work. The discoveries made with those resources are another outcome. More money might permit important research, but the size of the grant cannot tell us how much scientific value it produced. An institution can create a gap in resources and a gap in recognition without those gaps carrying identical information about achievement.

The comparison with the online comment is instructive. An artificial vote changes the evaluation of fixed words. A grant can help change the future words, experiments, and projects themselves. Both involve feedback, but the productive consequences need not be the same. Understanding cumulative advantage requires following what the reward does after it is received, rather than treating attention, money, equipment, and achievement as interchangeable quantities.

### What failure selects

A story about early advantage should be able to survive an encounter with a successful loser. In a 2019 study, Yang Wang, Benjamin Jones, and Dashun Wang compared junior scientists whose applications narrowly received or missed US National Institutes of Health funding. Early rejection was associated with greater departure from the NIH system. Among those who remained active, however, near-misses subsequently produced work with greater citation impact than narrow winners.[^c1-6]

Among those who stayed, some did distinguished work after an early rejection. Others left the funding system. Judging the setback only by the survivors would omit those departures; judging it only by departures would omit the later achievements. Both groups were part of the original applicant pool.

For a scientist still submitting proposals, the ability to continue may be part of what distinguishes that person from someone who stops. Later success among survivors does not by itself show that giving everyone the same early setback would improve scientific work. The researchers investigated whether selection alone could explain their findings and argued that it did not fully do so.[^c1-6]

Imagine reading a shelf of papers by researchers who overcame rejection. The papers are real achievements. The biographies might offer useful accounts of persistence. But the shelf contains no place for papers that were never written by people who lacked the resources, inclination, or opportunity to continue. Admiring the work on the shelf and evaluating the institution's treatment of the original applicants are different tasks. One requires attention to achievements; the other also requires attention to absences.

This is the same discipline we needed for Lockdown's missing chart. An outcome can be impressive without revealing every route by which the observed group came to exist. A scientist's later work may genuinely be better. The additional question is what an observer can infer about a process that also affects who remains visible. The existence of a successful survivor cannot, unaided, make the cost of the missing careers disappear.

Nor should we turn the funding result into a convenient reversal of the Matthew effect. Further funding and citation impact measure different outcomes. A person can receive less money and produce influential work. Another can receive more money for research that is valuable in ways the chosen citation measure does not capture. The question of which reward predicts which achievement must remain specific enough for evidence to answer it.

Subsequent researchers tested both patterns across a wider set of funding programs. A 2025 reviewed preprint by Vincent Traag and colleagues examined fourteen programs from six funders in Europe and North America. It found the relationship between early and later funding more robust than the apparent advantage in later citation impact following rejection. The latter varied with setting and method; selection into reapplication offered an important explanation.[^c1-7]

That is useful news for anyone tempted by a universal story. A striking finding about one group can identify a mechanism worth studying without becoming a rule for all careers. Replication can change our confidence about how far it travels. If a comparison is valuable enough to influence policy, it is valuable enough to examine under arrangements that might reveal its limits.

For a funding agency, the practical choice is not whether to believe that winners are talented or that losers are resilient. It is how to allocate resources while learning what those allocations do. Does an early award change later applications? Does it help people do better work? Does a rejection remove promising candidates from future consideration? Different records are needed for the different answers. A single later honor roll cannot contain them all.

An early rejection need not settle a scientist's career. Another funder, laboratory or employer can provide a route back. People respond to setbacks, and institutions can revise earlier judgments. Which opportunities remain available, to whom, and for how long helps determine whether an early disadvantage persists.

### The road with the fuel

Long before online charts, Armen Alchian offered an economical way to separate a successful outcome from a wise decision. His 1950 paper imagined thousands of travelers leaving Chicago along randomly chosen roads. Gasoline stations existed on only one road. Travelers who happened to choose it could continue; the others would run out of fuel. The continuing travelers might look unusually far-sighted to someone who saw only their progress.[^c1-alchian]

Alchian was illustrating how an environment could select surviving behavior without requiring every individual to understand the selection in advance. If gasoline supplies moved to another road, the pattern of continued travel would change even without travelers changing their paths. This was an explicitly unreal thought experiment, designed to make a point about selection under uncertainty, rather than an account of actual motorists.[^c1-alchian]

The travelers help clarify what is at stake in a record of winners. Progress along the fueled road is a real outcome. It would be absurd to say that the travelers had not moved merely because their initial route was chosen randomly. But observing that progress would not establish that their original decisions were unusually well informed. The fact to explain and the explanation attached to it remain distinct.

In the grant studies, the scientist who keeps applying remains eligible for later awards. The one who stops no longer appears in the same competition. Their continued presence or absence says little on its own about the earlier experiences that changed their choices and opportunities.

The thought experiment also reveals a possible abuse of skepticism. An observer could insist that the successful travelers were only lucky and therefore dismiss everything they did afterward. That would explain too little if, in a richer example, continuing the journey allowed them to acquire knowledge and develop skills. An early advantage can create conditions for later productive activity. The existence of the advantage does not make that later activity unreal.

For a Wikipedia editor, recognition can bring another person to a useful contribution. For a business, revenue can finance improvements. These are different mechanisms. Alchian's example exposes an inference from survival; explaining a particular reward system still requires investigating how it selects, rewards, and develops its participants.

Markets can select viable activities under uncertainty. People can imitate, adapt, and learn without possessing a perfect account of the economy. Choosing a business that works is one task; measuring how much each input contributed to its rewards is another. The first can be useful while the second remains unresolved.

The response should be to examine what would discriminate between explanations. In the travelers' example, discovering where the fuel was located changes the interpretation of the journey. In the online experiments, the researcher's record of the assigned advantage changes the interpretation of the later score. In a real procurement process, a useful independent trial might do similar work. Each additional observation earns its value by answering a question the winner's progress could not settle alone.

We can therefore learn from a successful traveler without treating every mile as a certificate of foresight. The same discipline lets us learn from a successful company without turning every sale into a complete causal audit. It asks us to understand the selection process as well as the selected outcome, and to notice which comparisons that process leaves available for the next observer.

### The car the buyer can inspect

Akerlof's used-car model makes another kind of missing evidence easy to picture. A seller learns whether the car is good through ownership. A prospective buyer cannot distinguish its quality as well. The offered price reflects that uncertainty, and a good car's owner may refuse to sell at it. The information problem can remove good cars from the set the buyer gets to choose among. Quality and selection interact.[^c1-8]

The buyer's uncertainty helps determine which cars actually appear in this market. Inspecting only cars offered at the resulting price would not show the full population of cars their owners might have sold under different information arrangements. The missing good car matters even though no transaction involving it appears in the sales record. Akerlof's example illustrates how a market's rules of information can help select its observable contents.

The contribution problem I want to isolate can remain even after a different uncertainty has been resolved. Suppose, in an explicitly imagined extension, an inspection tells the buyer the condition of each car accurately. The hidden-defect problem in the example has changed. But that knowledge would not tell a dealership how much its advertising added to sales, how much a salesperson's work affected a customer's choice, or how a prior reputation brought that customer through the door.

This does not make inspections unhelpful. An inspection answers a valuable question about the car. It may enable a trade that otherwise would not occur. The mistake would be asking the inspection to provide an account of every input's effect on the dealership's income. A certificate of condition and an estimate of contribution are different products, even if both are called information.

eBay's paid-search study provides a real counterpart. There was no need to doubt that an advertisement had appeared or that a customer had clicked. The records could establish the event. The experimental suspension showed how much apparent advertising success could be supplied through another route instead. The question was about an effect, and the added evidence came from changing the conditions under which that effect could be observed.[^c1-9]

It helps to picture the two records side by side. In one, the customer follows the paid link. In the other, the customer can reach the same destination without that advertisement. We are interested in the difference in purchasing, rather than the mere existence of the first route. A detailed account of the first route is useful for running a website. The second arrangement is useful for investigating what the advertisement contributes.

Broader searches required another test. Someone typing “cell phone” need not have eBay in mind. Using Google's geographic bidding system, the researchers stopped nonbrand advertising for sixty days in 68 US market areas, selected using a random draw followed by matching on past sales. Sales could then be compared with places where advertising continued. New and infrequent customers responded more than frequent customers. An advertisement could be useful to a shopper without creating the purchase that its click would later appear to explain.[^c1-9]

This is where a familiar response to disputes about success reaches its limit. Show the work. Publish the records. Demonstrate that the product functions. Those are useful demands, and sometimes they settle the question actually being asked. But they establish contribution only to the extent that the observations distinguish the relevant alternatives. When the dispute concerns how much a factor changed an outcome, evidence that the factor existed can leave a substantial part unresolved.

In MusicLab, everyone could in principle inspect the same recording. The result did not require one world to receive a secretly defective version. The experimental setting separates the song from some of the history through which listeners encounter it. A recording can therefore be known and its possible market outcomes still varied. This is the observed-input question in a form that does not depend on concealing the product.

Making a useful product establishes an achievement. Measuring how much of its maker's income that achievement caused requires a further comparison. Respect for the work can coexist with uncertainty about the size of the reward attributable to it.

### What success makes possible

An early advantage can finance productive capabilities that become valuable in their own right. Experience with customers can improve a product; revenue can pay for research. Later success may then have better immediate reasons than the original allocation did.

The improvement does not identify its own origin. Opportunity and the response to it have acted together. Another recipient might have developed an equally useful capability, a worse one, or something different. Observing the actual improvement cannot choose among those possibilities.

The work performed along the successful path remains real. A useful explanation must account for both its productive effect and the circumstances that made it feasible. Labeling the first advantage luck cannot measure the later work away; documenting that work cannot establish what a different initial allocation would have produced.

Obtaining the missing comparison can be expensive. Switching systems, training staff, or running an experiment consumes resources and may interrupt reliable service. The economic question is which uncertainty matters enough to justify investigating, and whether a smaller test can resolve it.

Bell's patent opening makes the distinction between knowledge and opportunity concrete. The historical study found that access to inventions and access to customers were different constraints. Outsiders could receive permission to use knowledge while remaining unable to turn it into a market record in the obstructed sector.[^c1-10]

Recognition can invite attention to existing work; revenue can finance new capability. Both change the record the next evaluator encounters. Treating every later award as a fresh judgment on unchanged starting abilities misses how earlier awards shaped the conditions of judgment.

The grant studies reveal another response: participation itself can change after an early result. When preparing an application is costly, repeated rejection can make another attempt unattractive. The observed field of candidates is partly an outcome of the reward process, rather than a fixed list on which that process merely acts.

By then a directory of remaining suppliers may conceal as much history as it reveals. It tells the buyer who is available now. It does not show all the firms that could have developed under different earlier allocations. This is one reason concentration deserves careful description. A smaller field could reflect superior performance, productive scale, restricted access, discouraged entry, or several influences together. Counting the survivors cannot settle the account of how they became the survivors.

### Turning the chart upside down

Salganik and Watts devised a particularly revealing challenge to the idea that a displayed record simply reports what an audience wants. In another MusicLab experiment, published in 2008, they allowed a popularity order to develop and then reversed the displayed download counts in two new worlds. A song with many downloads was presented as having few; one with few was presented as having many. After this intervention, new downloads were recorded honestly.[^c1-11]

The swap could be remarkably concrete. Before inversion, She Said by Parker Theory had 128 downloads and Florence by Post Break Tragedy had nine. In the altered displays, their apparent counts were exchanged. No band needed to improve a recording for the screen to give later listeners a different account of its success. The researchers had changed the history people believed they were entering.[^c1-11]

The first MusicLab experiment followed independently developing histories under unchanged arrangements. The later experiment deliberately altered the popularity record to test how people responded. It changed the chart that listeners saw, even though the recordings stayed the same.

Some apparent popularity became self-fulfilling. Yet the inversion did not permanently reverse the market as a whole. The songs with the greatest independent appeal tended to recover their popularity. The manipulation also reduced overall downloads. The result supports influence while showing that influence encounters preferences and can affect how much people take from the market, as well as which song receives their attention.[^c1-11]

That recovery is an important part of the ending. The best songs were not helpless objects waiting for a number to tell them what they were worth. Listeners still heard recordings and responded to them. A useful argument about history needs space for those responses to overcome some disadvantages. Otherwise, it would have no way to explain why the inversion did less than a fully self-fulfilling account might predict.

The lower download total raises a separate question about the whole market. A band could benefit from a favorable display while listeners collectively found less music they wanted to keep. An individual's rank and an audience's total enjoyment need not move together. The experiment measures downloads rather than every aspect of welfare, but it makes clear why explaining who wins is different from evaluating the outcome for everyone affected.

For Lockdown's first chart, we now have several warnings against explaining too much from the ending. Initial support can attract further support. A positive vote can influence later evaluation. A grant can change who applies again. A manipulated chart can divert attention, and strong preferences can also resist it. The evidence gives us several mechanisms, rather than a single principle under which every winner is either inevitable or arbitrary.

The next step is to ask what the record itself can teach us once those mechanisms are operating. Suppose another thousand choices arrive. They might test something new, or repeat a route already strongly favored by the existing history. We need an account of the opportunities for informative comparison inside that sequence. Counting transactions is an appealing place to start, but it cannot be the end of the inquiry.
