# Chapter 12

## An economics of possible worlds

The winter wheat planted at Broadbalk in autumn 1843 was harvested in 1844. The records of that harvest were the beginning of a much longer undertaking. Fertilizers, manure, and untreated ground supplied contrasts that successive generations of researchers continued to study. The field produced grain. Keeping differences alive helped it produce evidence too.[^c12-1]

An ordinary commercial field might sensibly receive whatever treatment its owner expects to work best. Broadbalk had another purpose. Some ground continued receiving treatments whose value could be assessed against other ground. Giving every strip the apparently best treatment would have changed what the field could teach.

The field has two products: grain and evidence. Producing the second requires land, labor, records, and arrangements that preserve useful differences. The harvest serves one purpose; the knowledge can benefit people who never buy its wheat.

The long record also makes a difficulty visible. An experiment cannot remain useful simply by refusing to change. Weeds, soil acidity, new varieties, and changing farming practices can alter the question a comparison answers. Rothamsted's account of its experiments describes considered modifications alongside preserved treatment differences.[^c12-2]

By the late 1940s, some plots had become so acidic that comparing two nitrogen fertilizers no longer answered the intended question. In the separate Agdell experiment, acidity favored a disease that depressed turnip yields; the experiment required extensive modification in 1951. Researchers applied chalk to correct acidity. Broadbalk later received shorter-strawed wheat varieties and a revised cropping arrangement in 1968. Preserving a useful experiment required changing what happened on the ground.[^c12-2]

Repeated applications of manure and fertilizer altered the soils themselves, leaving different stores of nutrients and organic matter. Later investigations could study those reserves and their effects. Yesterday's treatment had become part of today's condition. A difference after many years can reflect the accumulated consequences of earlier management as well as the immediate application. Comparing this year's input, a sustained practice, or a change from the inherited state asks different questions.

A field trial compares treatments with accumulated histories. It cannot erase their earlier development. Likewise, evaluating available technologies and estimating how different early opportunities would have changed their development are different questions, with different evidence requirements.

There is no timeless instruction here to hold everything fixed. A researcher must know which difference is being maintained, what else has changed, and whether the resulting observation still bears on the intended question. A familiar treatment name cannot guarantee an unchanged experiment.

AI evaluation faces a similar continuity problem. An unchanged benchmark can become a precise answer to an obsolete question. Revising it only when a favored system performs badly can undermine comparability. Documented changes are part of the evidence.

Broadbalk's plan distinguishes cropping and management histories across treatment strips. The name alone does not identify the ground, treatment, or period producing an observation. The same discipline applies to software: a service label can survive changes in models, interfaces, and review procedures. Version history helps separate those changes from differences in assigned work.

The fields yield crops and measurements: harvests, soil conditions, and changes across treated plots. Maintaining those comparisons is part of Rothamsted's productive work. Keeping an experiment going can make questions answerable that an ordinary harvest would leave unresolved.

Economics studies how resources produce goods and services, how incentives influence behavior, and how uncertainty affects choices. The arrangements that let alternatives develop interpretable records deserve the same attention. Losing those arrangements changes what an economy can know about its own possibilities.

## A second use for the harvest

Rothamsted's archived samples can be examined with methods unavailable when they were collected. Physical material preserves possibilities that a table of old measurements alone cannot supply.[^c12-3]

Sarah Bearchell and colleagues powdered old leaves and stems using liquid nitrogen and extracted short DNA sequences, allowing for damage in the aged material. Their 2005 study examined two wheat pathogens in samples spanning 1844–2003. Gaps required combining material from two plots after checks of comparability. Changes in relative pathogen abundance were associated with sulfur-dioxide emissions. The researchers considered other correlated factors; atmospheric pollution had not been randomized.[^c12-4]

Someone had kept the samples before knowing that a future laboratory could ask them this question. The archive connected a new method to observations that could no longer be collected afresh. It could preserve material from a harvest that occurred. It could not supply the harvest from a treatment never applied to that ground.

Digital records have the same distinction. Requests, versions, outputs, corrections, and checked outcomes can support more questions than a success count. They cannot manufacture the response of an alternative that was never tried.

Preservation costs money and can expose sensitive information. A useful record retains the context needed for authorized questions, with limits on access and a reason for keeping it. Missing entries, forgotten selection rules, and changing definitions can make even a large archive hard to interpret. Keeping everything indiscriminately can burden people without helping a consequential decision.

The durable resource is the connection among the task, the choice, and what happened afterward. Later investigators can revisit that connection as their methods improve. Where the needed event never occurred, someone must create a new comparison.

## The arrangement makes a difference

Ronald Fisher's 1926 paper on field experiments began with a familiar difficulty: apparently similar ground can yield differently. Weighing the crop accurately does not establish how much a treatment caused the difference. The arrangement of the plots affects what the result means.[^c12-5]

Fisher examined how allocation and replication affect estimates of error. Appropriate random arrangements could support an inference that a systematic pattern of treatment labels could undermine. Broadbalk's original plots were not a modern randomized trial; its evidence comes from maintained contrasts, subsequent changes, and accumulated observations. Each record has to be understood through the process that made it.

A service comparison likewise needs tasks of known difficulty, suitable assignment, and trustworthy checks. A shared faulty reference can make several systems agree on a wrong answer. Repeating the evaluation does not repair that common error.

The comparison budget adds a specific quantity to this account of evidence: cumulative allocation chance outside the current favorite. Under the Chapter 3 conditions, it limits information about productive input effects in the allocation history. Customer outcomes, independent tests, or other informative measurements can supply evidence beyond that history.

An empirical application can examine a defined period without asserting that a market's lifetime budget is finite. Assignment probabilities need a documented basis and uncertainty where they are estimated. Completed-work shares cannot substitute for them. Paired performance measurements contain more information than a list of allocation winners, and the analysis must count it.

Simulation can test a method in an artificial market whose rules are known. Morris, White, and Crowther explain how explicit aims, data-generating mechanisms, and performance measures make such a study interpretable.[^c12-7] Connecting its findings to an actual economy requires evidence about that economy's rules. A successful recovery of an artificial parameter tests the method in that setting; a well-designed field comparison tests the real decision.

## What an empirical test would need

Kasy and Sautmann's agricultural recruitment application supplies a documented example of matching experimentation to policy choice. Six recruitment options were tested through an adaptive calling design with 10,000 numbers over seventeen waves. The outcome was completion of five recruitment questions, rather than later agricultural income.[^c12-9]

The call experiment measured recruitment. To assess the agricultural service's effects on yields or income, researchers would need to follow those outcomes as well. What gets recorded determines which decisions the evidence can support.

A useful study would distinguish assignment chances, observed outcomes, and the effect being estimated. It would ask what can be learned from allocations alone, how checked outcomes change the answer, and how sensitive the conclusion is to plausible feedback rules.

Repeated records from one event, common shocks, and shared evaluation errors can make observations dependent. Recording versions, exclusions, and failures helps later investigators judge how much independent evidence the record contains.

Feasibility includes power to detect a consequential difference, the cost of supervision, privacy protection, and what action the result can change. A spending limit controls expenditure; it does not by itself establish adequate information or net social value.

The resulting report should connect methods, uncertainty, costs, and decisions. Negative and missing results need to remain visible. A successful evaluation can favor an existing arrangement, recommend a narrower change, or show that further investigation is not worth its cost.

## The permissions behind the experiment

The design still needs an institution capable of carrying it out. Requests may be controlled by the buyer, service interfaces by suppliers, and verification resources by another organization. Having each ingredient somewhere does not mean the usable experiment has been assembled.

Kenneth Arrow's 1963 study of medical care supplies an earlier institutional argument. The patient, in his account, cannot inspect the service fully before consuming it and depends on knowledge the physician possesses. Expectations that the doctor should put the patient's welfare first help organize this unequal relationship. Arrow interpreted medical institutions partly as responses to uncertainty about illness, treatment, and information. He also warned that an attempted remedy can introduce other obstacles. Institutions deserve investigation as arrangements for managing uncertainty, with consequences that require appraisal.[^c12-arrow1963]

James Buchanan and Yong Yoon analyze an anticommons: multiple rights to exclude can leave a resource underused. Their parking example requires complementary permits from separate holders.[^c12-10] Information can face a related institutional problem when useful records, interpretation, and permission to evaluate are controlled separately.

Contracting can sometimes assemble those complements. Commitment, verifiable delivery, compensable costs, and financing determine whether a feasible agreement exists. A nominal right of access does not provide the remaining inputs for free.

This should make us wary of explaining every refusal as a plan to suppress competition. A holder may face genuine privacy, security, staff, or productive costs. The useful question is whether those costs exceed the attainable benefit, whether the beneficiary can compensate the provider, and whether the arrangement can be enforced. A refusal can be privately rational without settling the wider economic case.

It should also make us wary of assuming that a mandate supplies every missing input. A rule can require access while leaving nobody responsible for defining the outcomes or paying the evaluator. Another can fund a laboratory that lacks permission to observe the relevant tasks. A useful institutional design assembles the complements needed for the stated comparison.

Rothamsted's maintained fields and archive offer a historical reference for this less glamorous work. Treatments, measurements, material, and documentation must remain connected across time. An isolated bag of grain and a beautifully organized treatment register are different things. Their joint usefulness depends on retaining the connection between them.

Institutional alternatives include existing qualified evaluators, secure contracts, shared infrastructure, and independently verified private tests. Their costs and possible common failures should be compared before establishing a new public body.

An experiment must permit a result favoring the incumbent. An institution whose survival requires the challenger to win has compromised its evidentiary purpose.

Credibility also requires stable criteria, recorded revisions, and relevant rights of challenge. Independent review can test whether the evidence supports a conclusion; the institution acting on it remains responsible for its decision.

An existing internal test may already resolve the buyer's uncertainty. Paying for a duplicate study would then spend resources without changing the decision. A missing alternative warrants further investigation when a feasible comparison could supply useful evidence.

## When the leading arrangement fails

After a leading system fails, another team needs resources to investigate, permission to test a different approach, and freedom to report what it finds. Public computing can support that work while admission rules or restrictions on publication obstruct it. What matters is whether a workable alternative can get through those steps and into use.

A failure can be isolated by independent tests or reproduced across many services sharing a common checklist and restricted evaluation route. The number of brands says little about which situation applies. Robustness concerns the practical ability to expose the failure and develop a workable alternative.

An available rival is little help if a buyer cannot transfer the records needed to test it. A dormant supplier's records can shorten recovery when they remain usable. Existing private tests or a cheaper substitute may already provide the answer. Waiting time, permissions, expense, and lost productive capacity determine whether a new access arrangement improves on those routes.

Subsidized computing that produces unusable results still consumes money, equipment, and skilled work. Official checks can spread a common error or suppress a finding that would embarrass the sponsor. A costly application process can exclude the team that needs the resources. The value of keeping an alternative available has to exceed those costs, including displaced private experiments and slower initial development.

Faster recovery in one country could reflect better equipment, a deeper pool of engineers, or other institutions alongside the access policy. Those differences belong in the explanation. So do the applicants turned away and the findings kept from public view. A list of successful recipients leaves out the people who never reached a test.

## The evidence behind a recommendation

An economic account of a widely used service should establish its costs, performance, and reach, then examine who can challenge it. The decisive questions concern a specified contribution, an observation process, and an action the evidence might change.

A buyer predicting tomorrow's workload has a different problem from one estimating the benefit of changing suppliers. Blackwell and Dubins showed how forecasts can merge under specified assumptions. In the allocation model, such agreement can coexist with unresolved productive contribution.[^c12-11] Agreement about the next winner does not establish why the winner wins.

Decision value matters as well as statistical precision. Blackwell's decision perspective supplies a foundation for asking what information enables a person to do.[^c12-12] A precisely measured tiny difference may leave a purchase unchanged. Uncertainty about a rare, costly failure may justify a targeted test. Collection costs, incentives, and usable access determine whether that test is worth buying.

The report must also identify which alternative it studies. A shadow replay preserves underlying effects, initial conditions, and response rules while events unfold differently. Changing an access rule or a training policy changes the institution. Its effects depend on how people respond under the new arrangement.

A usable account can be brief: what was compared, how it was assigned and checked, what remains uncertain, and what decision follows. Detailed methods and authorized access to records let others challenge the answer.

## Economics should test the rules

A procurement study can follow one purchase: who was eligible, how the supplier was chosen, what was checked, and what the buyer learned. Comparing the information in the award record with the information added by performance checks gives the comparison budget a real empirical setting.

Then examine the route to another test. A buyer might retain better records, evaluate a rival on suitable tasks, or purchase an independent assessment. If an alternative cannot reach the trial, identify the missing permission or resource. A bounded change in access can reveal whether new work becomes feasible and whether it improves the decision. Private testing displaced by a public experiment belongs in the cost.

Hayek described competition as a way to discover facts not previously known or used.[^c12-13] That makes the institutions supplying discovery part of the economy's productive equipment. They determine whether a capable alternative reaches a test, whether findings reach the people who need them, and whether those people can act.

For AI, the missing resource could be computing, a power connection, skilled staff, or suitable tasks. A team capable of a useful evaluation need not reproduce an entire frontier training operation. The smallest workable comparison can be worth more than a nominal right to enter a market it cannot reach.

Public support must be answerable to those outcomes. A remedy that adds expense without usable evidence should change or end. A private test that already resolves the uncertainty should count. The result may favor the incumbent; an institution committed to discovery has to let that finding change its decisions too.

## Growth without a way to find out

Kauffman asked whether biospheres tend to expand the possibilities available to them as rapidly as their organization can sustain. This was a conjecture about the creation of new possibilities.[^c12-15] Once a possibility exists, an economy faces a further task: giving it a route to development and learning from what happens.

A service can become more capable while independent ways to test its alternatives become harder to sustain. Its owner's next investment improves the visible product. A rival without a route to customers cannot build the record that would reveal whether a different investment could do better. Growth in productive capacity and growth in the capacity to judge alternatives can come apart.

The comparison budget puts a defined part of that process into the information account. It connects chances outside the favorite to limits on what a reward history can reveal about productive input effects. The conditions in Chapter 3 identify a case where continuing that history cannot finish the attribution job. Evidence from another source can matter precisely because more of the same is insufficient.

At network scale, preventing a challenger from beginning can remove a future comparison before a conventional performance test is even available. Preserving discovery therefore means protecting feasible development routes as well as comparing finished products. A right to start that cannot be exercised leaves the established result in command of the next result.

## The record we choose to leave

At Rothamsted, someone had to preserve the samples, label them, and keep the treatment records intelligible. Later researchers could put new questions to that material. An archive that had been discarded could not have answered them.

An economy makes a similar choice whenever it allocates another opportunity. It can support the established supplier and also preserve an independent route through which someone else could demonstrate a better method. It can distribute a share of capital's returns more widely and give people resources with which to attempt something new. Or it can let control over the next attempt accumulate with control over the last reward.

The choice concerns who can produce evidence that the existing record does not supply. A potential challenger needs a workable beginning before there can be a finished product to compare.

An economy committed to independent discovery has reason to reward useful work while preserving routes to challenge it. A substantial return can finance the next invention. Giving its recipient authority over whether anyone else can attempt one adds a different power, with different consequences. The payment and the power should be judged separately.

Start with a gatekeeper controlling an indispensable route to evaluation. If closing it threatens a lasting loss of useful comparison, protecting access preserves the possibility of discovering which supplier deserves the next contract. Documented interfaces, voluntary portability, secure evaluation access, and independently reviewed terms can make an enforceable duty concrete. The obligation should follow the actual obstruction, with privacy, security, resource costs, and investment included in its design. When useful private evidence already answers the affected decision, duplicating the experiment may buy nothing.

Act while the alternative can still develop. A team cannot be required to demonstrate years of learning before obtaining the customers through whom that learning would occur. Before closure becomes difficult to reverse, a credible attempt needs a route to its first test. Keeping that route open lets customers judge the result without promising the entrant a contract or permanent support.

Where restrictive conduct prevents valuable learning and imposes a cost on others, leaving that cost outside the decision rewards too much restriction. An access duty may prevent the loss. A corrective charge may address a remaining external harm. Its justification depends on the missing comparison, the decisions affected, and what feasible replacement would achieve. A large profit supplies neither a damage meter nor a reason to ignore a demonstrated obstruction.

Opening a gate accomplishes little for someone unable to afford the attempt. Independent learning can benefit people beyond the buyer paying for it, leaving that buyer too little reason to meet its whole cost.[^c12-16] Financing shared capabilities spreads the bill for discovery and gives more people the means to participate. Capital income and inheritance taxation can support a shared computing facility, a first demonstration, or broader ownership of productive assets. The case rests on the opportunities those arrangements create. Investment effects, avoidance, administration, and the benefits delivered determine workable terms. An exact retrospective division of each fortune into merit and position is unnecessary for choosing them.

The capacity to begin something different should remain a shared opportunity. When private systems close practical routes to earning, building, and owning, protecting the owners' investment should carry a reciprocal obligation to share the returns. Everyone living under that arrangement should retain a stake in the prosperity it produces. Membership in society should establish that basic claim before an employer, investor, or evaluator judges what a person might be worth.

A universal social dividend makes that stake independent of a gatekeeper's approval. The worker who never receives a first assignment cannot show the skills that experience might have developed. The founder who never reaches a customer cannot show the business those sales might have sustained. Their payment should not depend on reconstructing those missing careers. It should extend equally to someone who chooses to care for a relative, study, or never run a company. People should share in prosperity without having to win admission to someone else's plan for it.

The payment should be regular, unconditional, and universal; the tax contributions financing it should be progressive. Returns on broadly held productive assets should help sustain it. An adequate income floor requires durable funding, useful public services, and investment in the production on which the payment depends.

Ownership and entry require attention together. A dividend secures an income claim; workable access preserves a chance to challenge the arrangement producing it. Public computing can also become a gate if one official evaluator controls admission. Shared ownership, independent evaluation, published project-eligibility rules, and appeal should operate together. Government should be as open to a challenge to its preferred answer as a private gatekeeper is required to be.

MusicLab gave the same recordings more than one opportunity to develop a following. Lockdown finished first in one world and fortieth in another.[^c12-14] Its success was real in one history. Its neglect was real in the other. Neither chart could tell the whole story by itself.

We will rarely have both charts for an economy. We still have to choose how its rewards are taxed, which rights accompany ownership, and what opportunities remain available to people whose work has not yet acquired a record. The choice can rest on shared aims and practical evidence about institutions. It need not rest on a claim that today's distribution has finally established who deserves everything they received.

A successful economy needs reasons to keep producing and the means for other people to begin. Protecting investment, financing useful challenges, and limiting the power to close an essential gate serve those purposes together. Success can earn a reward. The next opportunity must remain open to a different answer.
