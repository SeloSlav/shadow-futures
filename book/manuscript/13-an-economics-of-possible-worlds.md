# Chapter 12

## An economics of possible worlds

The winter wheat planted at Broadbalk in autumn 1843 was harvested in 1844. The records of that harvest were the beginning of a much longer undertaking. Fertilizers, manure, and untreated ground supplied contrasts that successive generations of researchers continued to study. The field produced grain. Keeping differences alive helped it produce evidence too.[^c12-1]

An ordinary commercial field might sensibly receive whatever treatment its owner expects to work best. Broadbalk had another purpose. Some ground continued receiving treatments whose value could be assessed against other ground. Giving every strip the apparently best treatment would have changed what the field could teach.

That does not make the experiment an argument against productive farming. It makes its second product visible. A harvest is an outcome. A maintained comparison helps investigate an explanation. The labor, land, records, and organization required to preserve that explanation have costs and possible benefits of their own.

The long record also makes a difficulty visible. An experiment cannot remain useful simply by refusing to change. Weeds, soil acidity, new varieties, and changing farming practices can alter the question a comparison answers. Rothamsted's account of its experiments describes considered modifications alongside preserved treatment differences.[^c12-2]

By the late 1940s, some plots had become so acidic that comparing two nitrogen fertilizers no longer answered the intended question. In the separate Agdell experiment, acidity favored a disease that depressed turnip yields; the experiment required extensive modification in 1951. Researchers applied chalk to correct acidity. Broadbalk later received shorter-strawed wheat varieties and a revised cropping arrangement in 1968. Preserving a useful experiment required changing what happened on the ground.[^c12-2]

The guide describes another consequence of persistence. Repeated applications of manure and fertilizer altered the soils themselves, leaving different stores of nutrients and organic matter. Later investigations could study those reserves and their effects. Yesterday's treatment had become part of today's condition. This is a productive reason to keep the history, rather than assume every harvest starts with identical ground. It also limits a careless reading of the result: a difference after many years can reflect the accumulated consequences of earlier management as well as the immediate application. The relevant comparison depends on whether the question concerns this year's input, a sustained practice, or a change from the inherited state.

A field trial compares treatments with accumulated histories. It cannot erase their earlier development. Likewise, evaluating available technologies and estimating how different early opportunities would have changed their development are different questions, with different evidence requirements.

There is no timeless instruction here to hold everything fixed. A researcher must know which difference is being maintained, what else has changed, and whether the resulting observation still bears on the intended question. A familiar treatment name cannot guarantee an unchanged experiment.

AI evaluation faces a similar continuity problem. An unchanged benchmark can become a precise answer to an obsolete question. Revising it only when a favored system performs badly can undermine comparability. Documented changes are part of the evidence.

Broadbalk's plan distinguishes cropping and management histories across treatment strips. The name alone does not identify the ground, treatment, or period producing an observation. The same discipline applies to software: a service label can survive changes in models, interfaces, and review procedures. Version history helps separate those changes from differences in assigned work.

The fields yield crops and measurements: harvests, soil conditions, and changes across treated plots. Maintaining those comparisons is part of Rothamsted's productive work. Keeping an experiment going can make questions answerable that an ordinary harvest would leave unresolved.

The future of economics needs more attention to that supply. We already study how resources produce goods and services, how incentives influence behavior, and how uncertainty affects choices. We should also investigate which arrangements let consequential alternatives develop interpretable records, and what is lost when those arrangements disappear.

## A second use for the harvest

Rothamsted's sample archive gives this argument an unexpected extension. Material retained from earlier harvests can be examined with methods unavailable when it was collected. The preserved record includes physical samples, not only numbers in a table. A later researcher can sometimes ask the material a new question.[^c12-3]

Sarah Bearchell and colleagues took small portions of old leaves and stems from archive containers, powdered them using liquid nitrogen, and extracted DNA. They looked for short DNA sequences to accommodate damage in the old material. Their 2005 study examined two wheat pathogens in samples spanning 1844–2003. Gaps in the archive required combining material from two plots, after checks of comparability. Changes in the pathogens' relative abundance were associated with sulfur-dioxide emissions. The researchers considered other correlated factors; they had not randomized atmospheric pollution.[^c12-4]

The useful surprise is the question made possible. Harvest material retained for one scientific purpose could contain evidence relevant to another. The archive connected a modern laboratory method with a sequence of older observations. It did not manufacture those earlier samples after discovering that they would be useful.

This gives preservation a potential value that cannot always be specified completely in advance. Someone designing a record for today's decision may not know every future question. Keeping suitable material and documentation can make later investigation possible. The archive supplies a real example, rather than a promise that every retained dataset will eventually justify its storage bill.

The distinction between storage and comparison remains essential. A sample can reveal another measurement of a harvest that occurred. It cannot supply the harvest from a treatment that was never applied to that ground. Preserving material expands the observations available from a history; preserving treatment contrasts helps create a different part of the evidence.

Digital records likewise differ in what they preserve. Requests, versions, outputs, corrections, and checked outcomes can support more questions than a success total. They still cannot invent an unobserved alternative's response to a task.

Neither statement is a reason to collect everything. Residents' records can contain sensitive information. Storage, documentation, security, and authorized access consume resources. The institution must choose what to preserve for justified purposes and how to protect it. A comparison worth maintaining may need a carefully limited record rather than unrestricted retention.

Preservation has an option value and a cost. Retention policies should specify permitted uses, provenance, exclusions, and limits on later claims while protecting the people whose data make investigation possible. The future researcher is one beneficiary among others.

The institutional task is to make those tradeoffs reviewable. A preservation decision should identify the anticipated use, likely costs, permissions, review date, and consequences of deletion. Some judgments will prove mistaken as technology changes. A documented reason for the original choice gives later decision makers a basis for revision. At Rothamsted, the connection between retained material and recorded management allows new questions to reach backward. In software procurement, a comparable ambition is a protected record that remains intelligible after an interface, model, or contractor changes. Neither ambition requires promising that every future question will be answerable.

The wheat archive also cautions against treating old records as self-explanatory. Researchers had to use methods suited to aged material and check what their measurements meant. An economist recovering old procurement logs would likewise need to understand missing entries, changing definitions, and the process that created the archive. Historical depth is valuable when it remains interpretable.

A final success label can discard the original input and the correction needed to diagnose a failure. Retaining those records may enable re-evaluation, subject to reliable checks, relevance, privacy, and reproducible software versions. A useful archive preserves enough context for a justified question.

Archives, natural variation, additional measured outcomes, and designed experiments can all supply evidence about an alternative. Each route depends on a connection between the observations available and the question being asked.

## The arrangement makes a difference

Ronald Fisher's 1926 paper on field experiments began with a familiar agricultural difficulty: apparently similar ground can yield differently. A treatment's success cannot be assessed solely by weighing the crop accurately. The arrangement of the comparison affects what the difference means.[^c12-5]

His discussion moved from replication to the way plots were allocated. A collection of treatment labels arranged systematically could produce a misleading estimate of error. Random arrangements, including appropriate restrictions that the analysis respects, could support a different inferential claim. The method depended on how the experiment was generated, rather than the appearance of its final diagram alone.

The original Broadbalk experiment should not be retroactively described as a modern randomized trial. Its lasting treatment contrasts, subsequent changes, and accumulated observations require their own interpretation. Fisher's paper supplies a separate methodological point: design is part of the evidence, and uncertainty calculations must correspond to that design.

The same design issues recur in service evaluation. Different task difficulty, inconsistent judgment, or a shared faulty reference can distort apparently balanced comparisons. Repetition alone does not repair a common measurement error.

The remedy is more specific than asking for more data. Which requests entered? How were they allocated? Which outcomes were independently checked? Which service version produced them? What could the system retain or learn between requests? The answers describe the experiment that the analyst actually has.

Fisher's question about apparently similar ground remains useful for economics: what would justify attributing an observed difference to a treatment rather than the allocation process? Designed experiments, credible existing variation, and structural restrictions offer different routes. Counting an outcome accurately does not identify its cause.

Experimental design, causal inference, structural modeling, sequential learning, and partial identification already provide ways to study counterfactual questions. The comparison budget focuses attention on one part of the evidence supply: the accumulated chances that an allocation goes outside the current favorite.

Under the model's conditions explained in Chapter 3, those chances limit how precisely productive contribution can be recovered from the allocation history. A researcher can then ask whether the real market supplies other observations that overcome that limit.

An application should start with the available records. Does the analyst know the assignment probabilities? Are customer outcomes, independent tests, or other measurements available? Those observations determine whether the model describes the actual information problem.

An empirical study need not begin by claiming that an industry's lifetime budget is finite. A finite record cannot establish that conclusion merely because comparison appears to be slowing. It can investigate a defined horizon, assess uncertainty about the allocation mechanism, and compare feasible ways of producing evidence. That would already be useful progress.

Simulation offers a narrower test. Build an artificial market whose rules are known, run the proposed estimator, and check whether it recovers what was put in. Tim Morris, Ian White, and Michael Crowther explain why a simulation study must state its aims, data-generating mechanisms, and measures of performance. A method can work in that artificial world while failing in a real market whose feedback rules are different.[^c12-7]

A credible account of an allocation process records eligibility, assignment, internal testing, rejected cases, and checked outcomes. Known probabilities should have a documented basis; estimated probabilities need uncertainty. Completed-work shares cannot substitute for them.

An experiment observing paired outcomes contains more information than an allocation-winner record. Its number of cases is not automatically a comparison budget. Applying an allocation-only limit while ignoring checked responses misstates the observation scheme. Designing additional informative outcomes can change what is learnable even when production remains concentrated.

## What an empirical test would need

Kasy and Sautmann's agricultural recruitment application supplies a documented example of matching experimentation to policy choice. Six recruitment options were tested through an adaptive calling design with 10,000 numbers over seventeen waves. The outcome was completion of five recruitment questions, rather than later agricultural income.[^c12-9]

The call experiment measured recruitment. To assess the agricultural service's effects on yields or income, researchers would need to follow those outcomes as well. What gets recorded determines which decisions the evidence can support.

A useful study would distinguish assignment chances, observed outcomes, and the effect being estimated. It would ask what can be learned from allocations alone, how checked outcomes change the answer, and how sensitive the conclusion is to plausible feedback rules.

Dependence belongs in that account. Repeated records from the same underlying event, common shocks, and shared evaluation errors cannot be treated as fresh independent evidence without justification. Recording versions, exclusions, and failures makes those judgments inspectable.

Feasibility includes power to detect a consequential difference, the cost of supervision, privacy protection, and what action the result can change. A spending limit controls expenditure; it does not by itself establish adequate information or net social value.

The resulting report should connect methods, uncertainty, costs, and decisions. Negative and missing results need to remain visible. A successful evaluation can favor an existing arrangement, recommend a narrower change, or show that further investigation is not worth its cost.

## The permissions behind the experiment

The design still needs an institution capable of carrying it out. Requests may be controlled by the buyer, service interfaces by suppliers, and verification resources by another organization. Having each ingredient somewhere does not mean the usable experiment has been assembled.

Kenneth Arrow's 1963 study of medical care supplies an earlier institutional argument. The patient, in his account, cannot inspect the service fully before consuming it and depends on knowledge the physician possesses. Expectations that the doctor should put the patient's welfare first help organize this unequal relationship. Arrow interpreted medical institutions partly as responses to uncertainty about illness, treatment, and information. He also warned that an attempted remedy can introduce other obstacles. Institutions deserve investigation as arrangements for managing uncertainty, with consequences that require appraisal.[^c12-arrow1963]

James Buchanan and Yong Yoon analyze an anticommons: multiple rights to exclude can leave a resource underused. Their parking example requires complementary permits from separate holders.[^c12-10] Information can face a related institutional problem when useful records, interpretation, and permission to evaluate are controlled separately.

Contracting can sometimes assemble those complements. Commitment, verifiable delivery, compensable costs, and financing determine whether a feasible agreement exists. A nominal right of access does not provide the remaining inputs for free.

This should make us wary of explaining every refusal as a plan to suppress competition. A holder may face genuine privacy, security, staff, or productive costs. The useful question is whether those costs exceed the attainable benefit, whether the beneficiary can compensate the provider, and whether the arrangement can be enforced. A refusal can be privately rational without settling the wider economic case.

It should also make us wary of assuming that a mandate supplies every missing input. A rule can require access while leaving nobody responsible for defining the outcomes or paying the evaluator. Another can fund a laboratory that lacks permission to observe the relevant tasks. A useful institutional design assembles the complements needed for the stated comparison.

Rothamsted's maintained fields and archive offer a historical reference for this less glamorous work. Treatments, measurements, material, and documentation must remain connected across time. An isolated bag of grain and a beautifully organized treatment register are different things. Their joint usefulness depends on retaining the connection between them.

Institutional alternatives include existing qualified evaluators, secure contracts, shared infrastructure, and independently verified private tests. Their costs and possible common failures should be compared before establishing a new public body.

An experiment must permit a result favoring the incumbent. An institution whose survival requires the challenger to win has compromised its evidentiary purpose.

Credibility also requires stable criteria, recorded revisions, and relevant rights of challenge. Independent review can test whether the evidence supports a conclusion; the institution acting on it remains responsible for its decision.

The same principle applies to the researcher. A study of comparison opportunities should preserve evidence that a market already generates useful tests or that no practical alternative meets the requirements. Negative findings help distinguish a mechanism from a slogan. They can show where the comparison-budget perspective adds little to an existing explanation.

## When the leading arrangement fails

The country comparison proposes a question about robustness: can an economy discover and adopt workable alternatives after a technological, commercial, or policy failure, at acceptable cost? A large computing fleet alone cannot answer it.

A failure can be isolated by independent tests or reproduced across many services sharing a common checklist and restricted evaluation route. The number of brands says little about which situation applies. Robustness concerns the practical ability to expose the failure and develop a workable alternative.

Track the route from discovering the problem to putting a better arrangement into service: permissions, time, expense, mistaken decisions, and productive capacity lost. Ask whether a dormant supplier's retained records helped, whether existing private tests supplied the answer, and whether a cheaper substitute was available. Then investigate whether public access, an enforceable interface, or transferable records improved recovery. Differences between countries would also require attention to their resources and other institutions; a faster recovery by itself would not identify the cause. Record unsuccessful applications too; successful recipients alone cannot describe the access system.

The hypothesis must be able to lose. Subsidized computing might produce unusable results. Official checks might suppress failures or spread a common error. Compliance burdens might deter the investment an alternative needs. Count displaced private experiments and the benefits of faster initial development as well as recovery. The relevant time horizon and consequences must be stated.

Announced American, European, and Chinese policies open this inquiry; they cannot settle it. Begin with comparable decisions and observable routes through failure. Ask who could challenge the accepted explanation, what evidence they obtained, and whether acting on it improved the outcome. Those questions let the institutional argument risk an answer against itself.

## What an economist should report

An economic account of a widely used service should establish its costs, accuracy, and reach, then explain what its operating arrangement permits participants to learn about alternatives. Productive improvement and comparative learning can move together or apart.

An informative report would state its target. Is it estimating the value of a supplier's recommendation, the effect of access on entry, the effect of a training design on workers, or the effect of an input on winning rewards? These questions can be connected, but a success on one is not an answer to all the others.

It would identify the observation process. The routing rule, the outcomes retained, internal experiments, and external tests can all matter. A conclusion drawn from allocation winners alone should say so. If more information exists, the analyst should explain what changes when it is included.

It would report the relevant uncertainty about the mechanism. Two histories with the same market shares may have different assignment processes. Two models can forecast the observed process similarly while disagreeing about how an intervention would work. Precise estimates under one maintained explanation should not conceal uncertainty about that explanation.

Blackwell and Dubins show how conditional forecasts can merge under specified assumptions. In the allocation model, that agreement can coexist with unresolved contribution. Accurate prediction of the next winner does not automatically identify the input effect behind the process.[^c12-11]

Forecasts of workload and inference about a different technology answer different questions. Each can be valuable when its evidence and assumptions fit the intended use.

It would also distinguish statistical information from decision value. A measured contrast could be precise but economically trivial. Another could remain uncertain while affecting a consequential choice. Blackwell's decision perspective supplies a foundation; collection costs, interpretation, incentives, and legal feasibility still determine whether obtaining the evidence is worthwhile.[^c12-12]

A precise but tiny contrast may leave an action unchanged; uncertainty about a costly failure may justify a targeted test. The report must identify the consequential decision rather than substitute a larger comparison budget for a value argument.

Finally, the report would state which counterfactual it is evaluating. A shadow replay holds underlying effects, initial conditions, and response rules fixed while allowing the realized history to differ. A new access rule or training policy changes the institution. Estimating the consequences requires assumptions about behavior under that intervention. The two operations should not slide into each other halfway through the argument.

A useful procurement report can be brief: what question did the buyer ask, which services were compared, what happened, what remains uncertain, and what decision follows? The methods and records should be available for anyone authorized to check them.

## A research program with room to fail

The comparison budget is not a ready-made ranking of economies. Application requires a specified allocation process, justified conditional probabilities, relevant alternatives, and a target the available observations can distinguish.

A first useful project could reconstruct a procurement process with known assignment rules and checked outcomes. It would ask how much the allocation record alone reveals, what the additional outcomes contribute, and how conclusions change under plausible feedback mechanisms. An estimate that changes sharply when a reasonable mechanism is altered should be reported as sensitive, rather than polished into false certainty.

A second could compare feasible evaluation designs within that process. Extend one history, create a fresh task cohort, retain a previous measurement, or buy another source of evidence. The aim would be to discover which option improves a defined inference or purchasing decision at acceptable cost. Calling a cohort fresh would not establish independence if it retains the same suppliers' learned state and shared shocks.

A third could investigate the institution supplying access. Which permissions, costs, and incentives prevent a useful test? Can a contract solve the problem? Do public tests replace private ones? Does a narrow interface preserve scale benefits while making a missing outcome observable? These are questions for evidence, and their answers could weaken as well as strengthen a case for intervention.

A useful next study could begin with one purchase. Record which alternatives were eligible, how they were selected, which outcomes reached the buyer, and what additional test changed the decision. That would not measure an entire economy’s lost possibilities. It could establish something more modest and actionable: whether a specific institution could learn more, at a cost worth paying.

Hayek described competition as a way to discover facts that were not known or used beforehand.[^c12-13] We can investigate where that discovery works, which questions it answers, and whether another arrangement would produce more useful evidence at acceptable cost.

Likewise, acknowledging the costs of duplicated production should not end the inquiry before considering duplicated evaluation. Several firms need not each build a complete frontier training operation to contribute useful tests of deployed services. Some comparisons can occur within an integrated organization; others may need independent access. The capability, cost, and information questions must each be examined.

The physical AI economy makes this separation consequential. Computing capacity, power connections, skilled staff, and working systems are productive constraints. Preserving a hypothetical competitor with no feasible route to meeting them is not an experiment. A bounded test of an available alternative might be. The researcher must specify the alternative well enough for somebody to attempt it.

The prospect of failure is part of the program's value.

A convincing first application should therefore be small enough to inspect. An observation inventory, an auditable allocation rule, a set of checked outcomes, and a plainly stated decision would be more valuable than an industry ranking built from convenient public shares. Other researchers could then examine the same target under a different mechanism, add an omitted measurement, or run a feasible replication. If replacing a lost comparison costs little, a dramatic damage story weakens. If a proposed intervention destroys more productive value than its evidence can recover, the recommendation should change.

There is a corresponding possibility on the other side. A seemingly efficient arrangement may omit the only feasible route through which an alternative can demonstrate a consequential capability. Discovering that fact could justify a well-targeted institutional change. The policy case becomes stronger when the missing comparison and the attainable improvement are shown, rather than merely imagined.

## The record we choose to leave

At Rothamsted, someone had to preserve the samples, label them, and keep the treatment records intelligible. Later researchers could put new questions to that material. An archive that had been discarded could not have answered them.

Markets leave records too. Some come from ordinary sales; others from deliberate tests. A customer database, a trial's assignment log, and an independent evaluation can help answer different questions. Economics should investigate what each costs to produce and which decisions it can improve.

MusicLab gave the same recordings more than one opportunity to develop a following. Lockdown finished first in one world and fortieth in another.[^c12-14] Seeing both charts changes the explanation we can give. An account built around the winning result has to accommodate the other outcome too.

To judge an institution, follow a decision. A buyer considering a new supplier should be able to use a trial's result. A training program should be able to change when a different method helps beginners. Keeping records, arranging access, and checking outcomes matter when they give someone a workable reason to act.

The value of another comparison lies in the choice it can improve. Economics should help us find where that value is greatest, and how to obtain it.
