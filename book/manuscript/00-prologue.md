# Prologue

## The other life of a song

In one version of a small online music market, a song called Lockdown finished first. In another, it finished fortieth out of forty-eight. The band was called 52Metro. The recording competing for attention was the same.[^1]

Consider how differently we might explain those results if we could see only one of them. First place would invite an account of what the band understood about its audience. Fortieth would invite an account of what it had failed to understand. Someone could listen carefully to the recording, find things to praise or criticize, and produce a convincing explanation either way. The chart would supply the ending. The explanation could be arranged around it.

We do this without intending to deceive. A result gives us somewhere to begin. Once we know who won, we can examine the winner's decisions, notice the good ones, and trace a route from those decisions to success. It is harder to inspect the equally plausible route that never became the story. Usually, there is no second chart on which the same recording finished somewhere else.

Imagine being offered a contract on the strength of that first chart. The buyer would have evidence of an audience. You would have evidence that your work had reached people. Both would be entitled to take those facts seriously. A contract, however, needs a decision about what to do next. An account of why the first chart took its particular shape needs something else. We can have enough evidence to make a sensible commercial decision while lacking enough to give a complete causal explanation of the success on which that decision rests.

For Lockdown, the researchers could inspect that second chart.

The researchers Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds, and Duncan Watts created MusicLab, an artificial cultural market whose results appeared in Science in 2006. Across two experiments, 14,341 participants encountered forty-eight unfamiliar songs. Some made choices without seeing previous download totals. Others could see them. The latter participants were randomly assigned to eight separate worlds, each accumulating its own downloads. All songs began with none.[^2]

These were different listeners, not the same people instructed to repeat themselves. The researchers had created separate histories, rather than eight chances to watch one audience make an identical decision. A choice in one world did not add to a song's displayed popularity in another. A listener arriving later encountered the history of the world to which that listener had been assigned.[^1]

That small separation matters. Imagine arriving at an unfamiliar restaurant with time to order one dish. A recommendation from someone who has eaten there can be useful. So can a list of popular orders. Neither guarantees satisfaction, but both offer a reasonable place to start. You need not be especially suggestible to take account of what other customers have chosen. You may simply want dinner without conducting a survey of the entire menu.

Now imagine that the restaurant displays every order as it comes in. An early customer chooses a dish for a reason peculiar to that customer. The next person sees that someone has chosen it. Another order makes the dish look a little more established. Some later diners try it and enjoy it. Their enjoyment is real. Yet the visible record now combines information about the dish with information about how it came to receive attention.

The difficulty is not deciding whether these diners have preferences. They plainly do. The difficulty is deciding what their accumulated choices tell an observer about a dish that fewer people tried. Perhaps the favorite is much better. Perhaps it is a little better and enjoyed an early advantage. Perhaps another dish would have become the favorite if the first customers had ordered differently. Each explanation concerns something beyond the final list of orders.

MusicLab made part of that difficulty observable. Stronger social influence produced more unequal success and greater variation between worlds. Quality still mattered: the most appealing songs, as assessed without popularity information, rarely did badly, while the least appealing rarely did very well. Between those extremes, outcomes varied substantially.[^2]

There is no reason to hear that finding as an insult to anyone who makes something good. A recording requires work whether it attracts a large audience or a small one. An overlooked song does not become badly performed because few people discovered it. A popular song does not lose its pleasures because its first listeners helped it along. The puzzle concerns the explanation of the gap between outcomes, not the existence of the work or the enjoyment.

Imagine being asked to explain a successful recording with an unusually complete file. You have the finished track and every earlier version. You can inspect the hours spent rehearsing, the equipment purchased, the revisions made, and the money put at risk. Suppose every entry is accurate. You would know far more about its production than an ordinary critic or investor. You would still need to distinguish the value of that work from the advantages created by its first successes.

One way to investigate would be to vary something and see what happened. Another would be to observe comparable beginnings that developed independently. The important point is that a more detailed production file and a useful comparison answer different questions. Evidence that an action occurred does not, by itself, measure the difference that action made. That difference depends on what would have happened under a specified alternative.

Economists call such alternatives counterfactuals. The word sounds more mysterious than the activity. When you wonder whether taking another job would have changed your career, you are asking a counterfactual question. When a buyer asks whether another supplier could have delivered the same service, the buyer is doing the same thing. The alternative needs to be possible and carefully specified. Otherwise, comparison becomes an exercise in giving our preferred outcome every advantage.

I use the term shadow futures for feasible market histories that could have developed under the same underlying economic conditions and rules, with different early events. A customer chooses another supplier. A first recommendation reaches another audience. Subsequent decisions respond to those beginnings. The histories can diverge even though the rules governing that response remain unchanged. These are the alternatives against which we would like to understand the history we actually received.[^3]

In a growing business, the divergence could affect more than attention. Consider a hypothetical supplier that wins an early contract and uses the proceeds to hire another engineer. That engineer helps improve the service. The next customer receives a better product. There is now a real productive difference between this supplier and a rival that missed the contract. Asking about the earlier opportunity does not erase the later improvement. It asks how the improvement came to be possible.

This is why the usual quarrel about luck and talent can become unhelpful. Both sides may point to something true. A favorable early event can change the conditions under which talent develops. Hard work can convert an opportunity into something valuable. Later success can then finance more work. We should expect these influences to interact. Sorting the completed outcome into separate piles requires comparisons that the outcome itself may not contain.

There is a practical reason to keep asking. Suppose a buyer wants to help more suppliers develop the capacity to deliver a difficult service. If the decisive bottleneck is technical ability, the buyer needs one kind of response. If the bottleneck is access to a first credible customer, another response might work better. The same eventual success can be compatible with different accounts of that bottleneck. An explanation matters because it guides the next decision, including the decision to leave a successful arrangement alone.

Long before MusicLab, Robert Merton described the Matthew effect in science: established researchers could receive disproportionate recognition, and recognition could help secure resources for further research. His account drew in part on Harriet Zuckerman's interviews with Nobel laureates. The relationship between achievement, recognition, and subsequent opportunity already had a substantial intellectual history.[^4]

Earlier still, in 1950, the economist Armen Alchian had explained how a market could select profitable behavior even when the people acting within it lacked perfect foresight. Successful selection did not require every participant to know the best course in advance. Realized profits could determine which activities survived.[^5] My question concerns what a history produced by selection allows us to learn about contribution when earlier rewards alter later opportunities. Those questions belong in conversation. Neither requires pretending that people know everything before they act. The additional difficulty is that the act of selecting a winner may change which evidence can be produced afterward.

Imagine a buyer choosing between two suppliers. At first, both have meaningful chances to demonstrate what they can do. After repeated awards, one has more references and a familiar relationship with the buyer. Continuing with that supplier may be sensible. Switching carries costs. But if almost every future order follows the established route, another order may reveal little about how much the suppliers' productive differences explain the result.

I call the accumulated chance of awards going somewhere other than the current favorite the comparison budget. Add those chances across successive opportunities, allowing the favorite to change. The total describes an aspect of the allocation process. It is not the number of times a rival actually wins, and it does not turn every award into an independent experiment. The productive differences must also be informative for the question we are asking.[^3]

Nor does counting names on a list tell us how much comparison remains over a relevant period. Two suppliers can appear side by side while almost every opportunity follows one route. Conversely, a single organization could preserve useful comparisons through informative trials. The question is what gets tested, under which conditions, and what the resulting evidence can distinguish. An alternative that never receives a meaningful chance contributes less to that inquiry than its place in a directory might suggest.[^3]

A market can have a favorite and continue learning about alternatives. A small continuing chance can matter greatly when it persists. The stronger problem arises in particular settings where opportunities outside the favorite dwindle so quickly that their lifetime total stays finite. In the models behind this book, that condition, together with the stated restrictions on the information available, can prevent reliable recovery of contribution from one growing allocation record. More time does not automatically supply the missing comparison.[^3]

That is a claim about what a history can teach us. It does not say the winner is unproductive. Nor does it mean that every other source of evidence is useless. Independent experiments or informative measurements may change what we can learn. The question must always specify which records are available and which effect we are trying to measure. An explanation becomes stronger by accounting for that evidence, not by assuming it away.

We can also become good at predicting an outcome without settling its explanation. In the hypothetical procurement market, an experienced observer might confidently predict that the established supplier will receive the next contract. The observer could be right repeatedly. But accurate predictions would not alone tell us how much of the supplier's position was caused by its productive advantage. Knowing where the next order is likely to go answers a different question from knowing what another beginning would have revealed.[^6]

It is tempting to think that a sufficiently detailed record must eventually resolve every reasonable disagreement about the past. Consider what a record of repeated purchases actually contains. It shows the transactions that occurred. Depending on what was measured, it may also show product performance and customer satisfaction. It does not automatically show how an unchosen supplier would have developed with comparable opportunities. That evidence requires an additional design or a defensible way to infer it.

Preserving that evidence is not free. A buyer who tries a less established supplier may receive worse service. Someone must bear that risk. A trial also needs a question worth answering, outcomes that can be interpreted, and a decision that can improve as a result. An economy cannot test every imaginable arrangement. But treating comparison as costly is different from treating it as worthless. We need to consider what a particular opportunity could teach us before deciding whether its disappearance is an efficiency gain, an acceptable sacrifice, or an expensive mistake.

This distinction becomes consequential when we leave songs behind. Suppose a public institution is selecting an AI service. It might compare answers to a common set of tasks, investigate reliability, and negotiate a price. Those are useful inquiries. It might also ask whether its purchasing arrangement will permit future comparisons, whether another supplier can build a usable record, and who will be able to evaluate the results. A cheaper service today and an informative purchasing process tomorrow are both things a buyer may value.

The same question reaches a worker whose first assignment becomes a reference for the next one. It reaches an entrepreneur whose first customers provide the means to improve a product. Eventually it reaches the public argument about who deserves which rewards. Real achievement can justify admiration without telling us the exact amount of income attributable to it. A society still has to choose incentives, honor legitimate commitments, and decide which differences in fortune it is prepared to accept.

Consider competition policy through this lens. Combining two routes to customers might save resources. It might also remove an informative comparison. Preserving both routes could be worthwhile, or an independent trial might provide the relevant evidence more cheaply. The comparison budget does not decide that tradeoff. It helps identify something that needs to enter the decision. Likewise, uncertainty about contribution does not calculate a tax rate. Moving from a limit on knowledge to a policy requires an account of benefits, costs, and the people affected.[^3]

MusicLab does not establish those conclusions about actual employers, AI suppliers, or tax systems. Its participants made choices in a deliberately simplified setting. A result from that setting cannot certify every later application. What the experiment gives us is an unusually clear opening: we can see the result of keeping separate histories available, then ask what an observer would have concluded if most of them had been hidden.

Try that last exercise with Lockdown. Put aside the world in which it struggled and look only at the one where it finished first. The listeners there had made their choices. Their downloads were genuine. An observer could investigate the recording and find reasons people liked it. Nothing about the alternative outcome would require denying those reasons. It would require reconsidering how completely those reasons explained the result.

Now retain the other chart. What had looked like an explanation of first place becomes a question about how the same recording could occupy such different positions. We can respect the work and the audience's pleasure while investigating how the market produced that particular ranking. The second history gives us a reason to ask for more evidence. If the other chart had been lost, what would we have accepted as proof?
