# Prologue

## The other life of a song

In one version of a small online music market, a song called Lockdown finished first. In another, it finished fortieth out of forty-eight. The band was called 52Metro. The recording competing for attention was the same.[^1]

First place invites a story. The band understood its audience. The song had something the others lacked. Listen again, knowing it won, and you might hear the qualities that explain its success. Fortieth invites a different story. The song failed to connect. Other bands understood something 52Metro did not. Listen again, knowing it lost, and its shortcomings might become obvious.

Usually, we get to tell only one of those stories. Someone succeeds, and the rest of us work backward. We examine the product, the strategy, the years of effort. The ending helps us decide which details mattered. We rarely get to check our explanation against another history in which the same work met a different fate.

For Lockdown, someone had kept the other chart.

MusicLab was a website where people could discover unfamiliar music. In research published in 2006, Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds, and Duncan Watts studied choices made by 14,341 participants across two experiments involving forty-eight songs. Some listeners saw no download totals. Others could see what earlier listeners had downloaded. These listeners were randomly assigned to eight separate worlds, each with its own evolving popularity record. Every song began with zero downloads.[^2]

A download in one world did nothing to the chart in another. Different groups of listeners encountered the same recordings, but accumulated different histories. In one experiment, popularity appeared as numbers beside songs in a grid. In the other, the socially influenced listeners saw a list ranked by popularity. Earlier choices could help determine what the next person noticed.[^1]

The stronger the social influence, the more unequal and unpredictable success became across worlds. Appeal still mattered. Songs that attracted listeners without popularity information rarely went from the very best to the very worst, or vice versa. But appeal did not dictate the finishing order.[^2]

Lockdown had acquired something more revealing than a good review or a bad one: another result. Its first-place finish was real. So was its fortieth-place finish. Any convincing account of its success now had to leave room for both.

The same question turned up a few years later among projects asking for real money.

On Kickstarter, researchers led by Arnout van de Rijt selected 200 new, unfunded projects. They gave an initial donation to 100 chosen at random. The first backer, whose presence later visitors could see, had picked a project by chance. The researchers then watched who else would put money behind it.[^3]

Among projects that received no experimental donation, 39 percent subsequently attracted money from other donors. Among those given the initial donation, 70 percent did. That later support excluded the researchers' own contribution. The first donation had helped bring others behind it.[^3]

An observer arriving afterward would see projects with several backers and projects with none. The successful pitches might seem clearer, their ambitions more compelling. Without knowing about the random donation, the observer could give the pitch credit for an advantage assigned by an experiment.

The researchers also tested larger initial advantages. Their effects showed diminishing returns: more manufactured success did not produce proportionately more subsequent success.[^3] An initial boost mattered, but increasing it did not keep buying the same additional response.

People can respond sensibly to a record of success while helping create the next success in that record. The later backers' money is real. The project may put it to excellent use. Yet counting those backers does not tell us how much support the project would have received without its experimental head start.

Return to Lockdown. Add another thousand listeners to the world where it finished first. We might learn much more about that audience's enjoyment. Those additional listeners would not recreate the world where the song finished fortieth. More observations of one history and more chances to compare histories are different things.

Markets do more than distribute money and attention. They help determine which alternatives get tried, which improve through use, and which produce a record anyone can inspect. A market can become very busy while leaving a particular question poorly answered: how much did productive work, rather than the path of earlier rewards, contribute to this success?

I call the cumulative chance of rewards going somewhere other than the current favorite the comparison budget. At each opportunity, take the chance that someone else receives the reward, then add those chances across opportunities. The favorite can change. This counts chances, rather than actual rival victories; it does not make every purchase or download an independent experiment. The question is how much opportunity the allocation process leaves for informative comparison.

MusicLab makes the distinction tangible. Forty-eight song titles can remain on a screen even while attention flows mainly toward a few. Keeping the titles available is different from getting the recordings heard. A long list of alternatives can coexist with a thin record of what they can do.

To investigate this question, I developed models of how markets distribute rewards. In these models, the comparison budget limits how precisely a reward history can reveal the effects of work, quality, and investment. Under specified conditions, opportunities outside the favorite can dwindle fast enough that even an indefinitely growing record cannot reliably settle attribution. A small chance that persists can still accumulate. The way those chances change over time matters, alongside their size today.

Success breeding success is an old idea. Robert Merton described how recognition could bring scientists resources for further work. Armen Alchian explained market selection under uncertainty decades before MusicLab.[^5] A lead can keep growing while opportunities to check its causes disappear. The growing reward and the shrinking capacity to explain it can be consequences of the same process.

Lockdown's other chart suggests what is missing. I call these feasible alternative histories shadow futures, borrowing words from Steven Johnson's account of Stuart Kauffman's adjacent possible.[^10] They share the same underlying economic conditions and rules, but early events differ. Later choices and effort can respond. The recording stays the same; different first listeners leave a different chart for everyone afterward.

Sometimes policy changes which alternatives can become real. In 1956, an American antitrust settlement opened 7,820 existing Bell System patents to royalty-free licensing. Bell Laboratories had helped create technologies including the transistor. Other businesses now had access to thousands of patented inventions without paying royalties.[^6]

Decades later, Martin Watzinger and his colleagues studied the consequences. Comparing Bell patents with matched patents outside Bell, they found a lasting increase in subsequent innovation outside telecommunications. They did not find the same increase within telecommunications, where access to customers remained obstructed. Permission to use an invention was not always enough to make a business from it.[^6]

The result gives the missing comparison a physical form: equipment that another company might build, offer, and improve. An invention behind a licensing barrier cannot tell us everything about what other inventors could do with it. Opening access changes the rules; it is an intervention, rather than a replay of an unchanged economy. But it can let possibilities previously confined to speculation develop observable results.

A restriction can prevent useful discovery before it raises a price. A rival may lose its route to a first customer, leaving no finished product for buyers to compare. Preserving that route can matter before there is a price dispute. Its value depends on what another supplier could test and what people could learn, as well as the costs of making the trial possible.

Preserving that route becomes more urgent when the thing being bought can help decide who gets hired, what work gets done, and which ideas receive attention.

Consider a customer-support worker with an AI assistant suggesting replies during a conversation. The worker can accept, edit, or ignore them. This was the setting studied by Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond, using data from 5,172 agents. Their published study found an average increase of 15 percent in issues resolved per hour after access to the assistant, with especially strong improvements among less experienced and lower-skilled workers.[^7]

Here is a reason to welcome the technology. A newer worker can draw on assistance that helps close a gap in experience. The study also found evidence of learning. AI can make useful knowledge more available to people who have had less time to acquire it. Any account of the future of work has to make room for gains like these.

The researchers examined periods when the AI system went offline and agents continued handling their customer conversations. Workers who had used the assistant still handled conversations faster than they had before gaining access. The pattern was stronger among people with more previous exposure to its suggestions. This was evidence consistent with learning: some benefit could remain when the immediate stream of advice stopped. A worker's improved performance could therefore reflect both what the software supplied now and something the worker had acquired through using it.[^7]

That interruption gives the comparison a concrete purpose. Watching a successful assisted conversation tells us what worker and tool accomplished together. Watching work continue during an outage helps investigate what the worker can do when suggestions disappear. Neither record alone describes every possible workplace. Together, they let us ask a sharper question about how a productive ability develops. The distinction matters when an employer decides whether software should guide beginners, handle their tasks itself, or change the training through which the next generation learns the job.

But a customer-support result cannot settle what happens in a software project, any more than Lockdown's winning chart can settle its fortunes everywhere. The work, tools, and opportunities to measure them differ. To see how quickly the comparison itself can change, follow what happened to researchers studying programmers.

METR, an organization that evaluates AI systems, ran an experiment in early 2025 in which experienced developers completed tasks with or without AI tools. In that setting, AI access made completion slower. When METR expanded the research later that year, it encountered a different problem: some developers did not want to participate if participation meant doing work without AI.[^8]

They were being offered fifty dollars an hour to work on their own projects. Still, some declined. Others held tasks back from random assignment because they did not want those tasks landing in the condition where AI was forbidden. In surveys, between 30 and 50 percent of developers reported withholding some tasks for that reason.[^8]

By February 2026, METR said the newer results gave an unreliable measure of the productivity effect. Changed pay and difficulties measuring time also complicated matters. Its researchers thought AI was likely helping developers more than before, while emphasizing how weakly their data measured the size of that improvement.[^8]

Think of a task the programmer keeps out of the study. It may be exactly the task on which AI saves the most time. The developer has a reason to keep using the tool, while the researcher has a reason to want the missing comparison. Both can be acting sensibly. The work gets done, but that opportunity to measure the difference has gone.

METR began redesigning its experiments. When developers change how they work, researchers have to change how they measure it. The question is how to preserve useful comparisons without asking people to abandon the tools they now rely on.

The same practical question runs from the programmer's desk to the data center. An artificial intelligence (AI) model is a computer system that uses patterns learned from data to make predictions or generate material such as text. Training an AI model means adjusting its internal settings using examples to improve its performance at a task, such as predicting the next piece of text. Running the trained model to produce an answer is called inference. Training, inference, and comparing one model with another are different activities.

A service can generate an enormous record of answers while leaving unanswered whether a different model, supplier, or organization of work would serve us better. AI can also make those comparisons cheaper. Which possibility prevails depends on how we deploy it.

The consequences reach the pay packet. Think again of the customer-support worker resolving more problems with AI assistance. The worker is doing useful work. So are the people who built the tool. The employer supplies an organization in which both can operate. Measuring the productivity improvement helps us understand what changed. It does not, on its own, tell us how to divide the additional income among them.

Now remember the Kickstarter projects. Their later support was influenced by a donation the researchers deliberately assigned. In ordinary business, we rarely have such a clearly marked intervention. We see the revenues after customers, reputation, investment, and opportunity have acted on one another. That makes the familiar claim that someone deserves precisely what the market paid them much harder to establish than the claim that they created something valuable.

Taxation enters here because governments must choose rules even when contribution cannot be divided neatly from position. Incentives matter; promises and legitimate entitlements matter. Uncertainty about a fortune's origins supplies no tax rate. It does require us to explain our tax rules without pretending that every pound or dollar arrives with a reliable account of who caused it.

When closing a route to customers prevents other people from learning something useful, part of the cost may fall outside the business making that choice. Access rules, public trials, or a carefully justified tax could address that loss. Their justification depends on what people have lost the chance to learn, how it affects their decisions, and whether the remedy is worth its costs.

We already know how to treat some comparisons as worth maintaining. At Rothamsted in England, agricultural experiments begun in 1843 have preserved differences in how plots are treated across generations. Soil and plant samples have been kept for more than 180 years. Some plots have received particular fertilizer or manure treatments since the beginning, apart from fallow periods. Methods have also changed to keep the experiments useful.[^9]

Those fields are productive in two senses. They grow crops, and their maintained differences help produce knowledge. Maximizing this year's harvest on every plot would answer a different question from the one the continuing experiments make possible. Someone has to value the comparison enough to keep it alive.

A song, a first donation, a patent, an AI assistant: each produces something people can value. Each also changes what happens next. The economy's rewards help decide which possibilities become familiar and which remain out of sight.

Lockdown finished first. Lockdown finished fortieth. The second chart survived because somebody built a place for it. The harder question is what happens to the futures for which we never make room.
