# AI Agents, Market Power, and the Futures We Never Test

*A new economic object—the comparison budget—asks what markets preserve the opportunity to learn.*

In February 2026, Anthropic researcher Nicholas Carlini described [sixteen AI agents working on a C compiler](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler). Their progress depended on carefully designed tests, including comparisons with the established GCC compiler. Producing more code became useful because there were ways to judge it.

That distinction matters far beyond software. An agentic economy, in which AI systems act on people's behalf, could generate extraordinary amounts of activity. Whether that activity teaches us which alternatives work better is another question.

Imagine purchasing agents repeatedly selecting an established supplier. Each successful delivery gives that supplier another reference, more revenue, and resources to improve. A competitor struggles to get its first meaningful trial. The agents might be making sensible purchases. The successful supplier might be excellent. Yet a growing transaction record could leave us poorly informed about what the competitor would have achieved with comparable opportunities.

This is the problem behind my book, *Shadow Futures*.

I call the cumulative chance of an opportunity going to someone other than the current favorite the **comparison budget**. Take those chances across successive opportunities and accumulate them. The favorite can change. The budget counts chances, rather than actual rival victories or the number of firms with a website.

The new economic object I propose connects allocation to information. In my specified model, the comparison budget bounds what the reward record can reveal about the effects of work, quality, and investment. Under further conditions, if that budget remains finite over the market's lifetime, even an indefinitely growing record cannot reliably settle contribution across all the candidate explanations.

A tiny chance repeated persistently can still accumulate without limit. Concentration alone therefore cannot establish this result. Relevant experiments and performance measurements must also count as evidence.

**Shadow futures** are the feasible histories that the same economy could have produced from the same starting conditions, underlying input effects, and response rules. An early opportunity goes elsewhere, and subsequent investment and learning develop differently. These alternatives help state the question that the winner's history cannot answer on its own.

The intellectual ancestry is substantial. [Armen Alchian, in 1950](https://doi.org/10.1086/256940), explained how profit-based selection could operate under uncertainty. [David Blackwell, in 1953](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177729032), provided a framework for comparing experiments by the decisions their information supports. [Blackwell and Lester Dubins, in 1962](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177704456), showed how forecasts can converge under suitable compatibility conditions as information accumulates. In my setting, that helps illuminate how predicting the next winner can become easier while explaining contribution remains unresolved.

[Kenneth Arrow's work on invention](https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c2144/c2144.pdf) exposed the difficulties of producing and allocating information through markets. [W. Brian Arthur's work on increasing returns](https://doi.org/10.2307/2234208) showed how historical events can shape technological lock-in. These traditions converge on a question: what evidence does a market produce about the process selecting its winners? My proposed contribution is a defined comparison budget and its information bound, building on those foundations. The technical argument is in my [SSRN preprint](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6003994).

AI raises the stakes because greater productive capacity can coexist with tighter control over access. A million agents do not automatically create a million independent routes into a market. Ownership of models and compute, procurement rules, and access to testing all shape what can be learned. AI can also make those tests cheaper and more informative. Which effect prevails depends on the arrangements we build.

That gives antitrust an informational question alongside its established concerns about prices, quality, and innovation: does a restriction eliminate valuable opportunities to discover an alternative? It also complicates arguments that treat income as a precise certificate of individual contribution. Any corrective tax still needs evidence of social costs and a workable instrument; uncertainty alone supplies no tax rate.

The image behind the book owes a debt to Steven Johnson's account of [Stuart Kauffman's adjacent possible](https://www.edge.org/conversation/stuart_a_kauffman-the-adjacent-possible). I want this argument to have an open future, too: one in which others can test it, improve it, and give it a wider audience.
